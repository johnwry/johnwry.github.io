Psalm 23

Notice how dynamic it is.

The shepherd:

leads makes lie down guides walks through prepares anoints

Then the worshiper:

walks is followed dwells

The entire psalm is movement.

It is difficult to read Psalm 23 as a static description.

It feels like a journey.

That creates a fascinating sequence:

The shepherd leads the sheep. The worshiper arrives at the house. The question is asked: Who may ascend? The qualified worshiper is described. The gates open. The King of Glory enters.

Before going further, I’d like to slow down over Psalm 24:3–6 because that section becomes crucial. The question is whether verses 3–6 are describing:

the worshiper entering, a class of people, the king himself, or something more complex.

The text itself gives us some clues.

The Structure Looks Like a Process

If we simply trace the verbs:

Psalm 23

Lead ↓ Walk ↓ Prepare ↓ Follow ↓ Dwell

Psalm 24

Ascend ↓ Stand ↓ Receive ↓ Seek ↓ Enter

That’s a remarkable amount of movement.

  1. Does Later Scripture Connect These Themes to Messiah?

Now things get interesting.

The Old Testament repeatedly joins together:

Davidic kingship shepherd imagery the LORD’s reign

Examples:

Psalm 2 Psalm 72 Isaiah 11 Ezekiel 34 Ezekiel 37

Especially Ezekiel 34 and 37.

There we find:

The LORD shepherds His people.

and

My servant David shall be shepherd over them.

The shepherd and king themes begin converging.

la escena de las ovejas ha quedado atrás.

Now we have:

  • host
  • table
  • cup
  • house

The destination is becoming visible.

Psalm 24’s Own Clues

Let’s stay in the text.

The final scene is unusual.

The King of Glory is not enthroned.

He is arriving.

Not:

The King sits.

But:

Lift up your heads…

that the King of Glory may come in.

The psalm depicts an arrival.

That is one reason many readers have sensed an event behind the scene.

The psalm is not merely defining a king.

It is announcing one.

Psalm 24:7–10 The King of Glory arrives.

Notice the overlap with Psalm 23.

Psalm 23: goodness follows me

Psalm 24: blessing from the LORD

Psalm 23: I dwell in the house of the LORD

Psalm 24: who may ascend?

Psalm 23: the LORD is my shepherd

Psalm 24: the God of his salvation

The themes continue rather than restart.

Something changes here.

Verses 3–5 used singular language:

he who has he shall receive

Verse 6 moves to a group:

this is the generation

Now the individual becomes a community.

The person who seeks God is not presented as an isolated exception.

He belongs to a larger company.

The second line expands the first.

The object of the search is not merely:

blessing righteousness protection

The object is:

Your face

The LORD Himself.

“Jacob”

Then comes the surprising ending:

Jacob

The psalm suddenly introduces Israel.

Not Abraham.

Not David.

Not Zion.

Jacob.

That is worth slowing down over.

The psalm could have ended:

This is the generation of those who seek Him.

But instead it adds:

Jacob.

The question becomes:

Why conclude the description of those who seek God’s face with the name Jacob?

Because immediately afterward the psalm turns to:

Lift up your heads, O gates…

that the King of Glory may come in.

So the flow appears to be:

7-10 The King of Glory enters

The text is moving from:

the qualified worshiper to the seeking generation to the arrival of the King.

The mention of Jacob acts like a bridge between the worshipers and the King of Glory section that follows.

The next question is whether the gates of verses 7–10 belong to:

the earthly sanctuary, Zion, Jerusalem, or something larger within the psalm’s own imagery.

The repeated dialogue (“Who is this King of Glory?”) gives us several clues.

The King of Glory arrives.

If that observation is correct, then the final emphasis of the section is not ultimately:

the table, the cup, the house, the mountain, or even the worshiper.

The final emphasis is:

“¿Quién es este Rey de gloria?”

“Jehová de los ejércitos, Él es el Rey de la gloria.”

And perhaps the biggest remaining question is:

Is Psalm 24 describing a timeless truth about the LORD’s kingship?

Or is it portraying a specific arrival of the King of Glory that later prophetic passages develop more fully?

is he king or in the king entering? Psalm 24 never says:

“The LORD is King.”

at least not directly.

Instead it repeatedly says:

“The King of Glory.”

and

“Who is this King of Glory?”

and

“The LORD of hosts, He is the King of Glory.”

Notice that the title is revealed through a scene.

The psalm does not begin with the title.

The title is unveiled at the end.

Observation 2

The most repeated action is not reigning.

It is entering.

Twice:

Lift up your heads, O gates…

that the King of Glory may come in.

Verse 7.

Then again:

Lift up your heads, O gates…

that the King of Glory may come in.

Verse 9.

The repeated action is arrival.

Not enthronement.

Not ruling.

Not judging.

Entering.

Observation 3

The psalm never describes the reign.

Compare with Psalm 2.

Psalm 2 explicitly discusses:

kings nations rule inheritance breaking with a rod of iron

Psalm 24 contains none of that.

It never describes what the King does after arriving.

The focus is entirely on His appearance.

Observation 4

The arrival creates the climax.

Suppose we stop at verse 6.

The psalm would read:

Who may ascend?

Who may stand?

Here is the qualified worshiper.

That would already be complete.

Yet verses 7–10 are added.

Why?

Because the psalm is not finished with the worshiper.

The climax is not the seeker.

The climax is the arrival of the King.

Observation 5

The King’s identity is progressively revealed.

First question:

Who is this King of Glory?

First answer:

The LORD strong and mighty,

the LORD mighty in battle.

Second question:

Who is this King of Glory?

Second answer:

The LORD of hosts.

The psalm seems more interested in unveiling who is arriving than in describing His kingdom.

Observation 6

The gates are commanded before the King arrives.

The sequence is important.

Not:

The King arrived.

Therefore open the gates.

But:

Open the gates.

Lift up the doors.

Then the King enters.

The psalm places anticipation before arrival.

A Tentative Observation

If I had to choose between:

Option A

Psalm 24 is primarily about the LORD’s kingship.

Option B

Psalm 24 is primarily about the arrival of the LORD as King.

The text currently seems to lean toward Option B.

Not because kingship is absent.

The King is explicitly identified.

But because the narrative energy is concentrated on:

“that the King of Glory may come in.”

The psalm spends four verses describing an entrance.

It spends zero verses describing the reign that follows.

That suggests the arrival itself is the focal point.

Now the next question becomes very interesting:

If Psalm 24 is emphasizing the arrival of the King of Glory,

where is He arriving?

The answer is not as obvious as it first appears.

The text never actually says:

Jerusalem Zion Temple

It only says:

gates

ancient doors

So before identifying the event, we should probably investigate the destination. That may tell us whether this is:

a liturgical procession, a temple entrance, a royal arrival, or something larger.

What Psalm 24 Does Not Say

Psalm 24 never says:

The LORD becomes King.

It assumes He already is:

The King of Glory.

Likewise Zechariah 14 does not describe God becoming king in the sense of acquiring kingship.

Rather, His kingship becomes openly manifested.

The emphasis is on appearance.

Another Connection

Psalm 24 asks:

Who is this King of Glory?

Zechariah 14 answers:

The LORD will be king over all the earth.

Both passages end with the LORD openly recognized as King.

The Jerusalem Question

This is where your observation becomes important.

Psalm 24 never explicitly names Jerusalem.

Yet several clues point in that direction:

Earlier in the psalm:

Who may ascend the hill of the LORD?

Who may stand in His holy place?

The “hill of the LORD” naturally raises the question of Zion.

Then later:

gates

ancient doors

Then:

the King enters.

If we read Psalm 24 by itself, we should stop there.

But once we compare it with Zechariah 14, a pattern emerges:

Zechariah 14

The LORD comes to Jerusalem.

↓

The LORD is acknowledged as King.

Psalm 24

The King comes in.

↓

The LORD of hosts is identified as King.

The movement is remarkably similar.

One More Observation

Notice where Psalm 24 places the arrival.

Not at the beginning.

At the end.

The entire psalm moves toward it.

Likewise Zechariah 14 moves toward:

The LORD will be king over all the earth.

The kingship declaration is the climax.

A Possible Synthesis

Without claiming dependence, the two passages appear to share the same broad pattern:

Psalm 24

Qualified worshipers ↓ Seeking His face ↓ Gates opened ↓ The LORD arrives as King

Zechariah 14

Jerusalem under pressure ↓ The LORD intervenes ↓ The LORD arrives ↓ The LORD reigns as King

The strongest observation may be this:

Both passages culminate not merely in the LORD’s authority, but in the LORD’s manifest arrival and recognition as King.

That pushes Psalm 24 slightly away from being merely an abstract statement of divine kingship and slightly closer to an arrival scene. The question then becomes whether later prophets such as Zechariah are intentionally developing the same arrival motif, or whether both are drawing from a broader biblical expectation of the LORD coming to reign.
