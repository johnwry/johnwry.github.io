---
title: Many books, one message
date: 2026/10/05
---

We live in a world where answers are at our fingertips. Type a question into Google, and pages of articles appear. Ask AI, and within seconds you receive an answer that sounds complete and certain.

But receiving an answer is different from knowing why we should believe it. Where did it come from? What supports it? How can we tell whether it is true?

Every day, we hear voices that shape what we believe. Science offers its latest discovery or a fun fact that catches our attention. Our news feeds tell us what’s happening and what we should make of it. A friend shares a Facebook post, and we scroll through shorts when we’re bored, picking up opinions along the way. Teachers explain things in class, while podcast hosts talk us through everything from parenting to the meaning of life. Politicians promise they have the answers. Religious leaders tell us what to believe about God. Friends and family chime in with, “Trust me, I’ve been there.” And our own feelings add, “I don’t know—I just feel like it’s true.”

Each voice carries some measure of authority in our minds. Sometimes we examine its claims carefully. Sometimes we accept them because they are familiar, widely repeated, or expressed with confidence. We may find ourselves believing an answer without ever examining its foundation.

Sometimes we discover how much authority we have given an opinion only when someone questions it. We feel challenged, perhaps even offended. What began as “Here’s what I think” has become “How dare you disagree?” A belief can become so closely tied to our sense of who we are that we defend it before we examine it. But how strongly we feel about something cannot establish whether it is true.

These questions become especially important when we ask about God, our standing before Him, and what lies beyond death. Whose testimony can establish the answers? And when the voices disagree, which one has the authority to correct the others?

Why should Scripture be the final authority for our faith and our understanding of this life and the life to come?

To answer that, let’s consider what Scripture claims to be.

God wants to communicate with us. He wants us to know who He is, understand our need, and receive what He has provided through Jesus Christ. Hebrews 1:1–2 tells us that God spoke through the prophets and has now spoken through His Son. The Scriptures bear witness to that Son, showing us who He is and what He has accomplished.

If Scripture is God’s written revelation, then its authority rests on the God who speaks through it. Our opinions must be examined in light of what He says—including the opinions we hold about Scripture itself.

So let’s open the Bible and listen. As we move through these passages, watch how the writers connect their message to those who came before them. Jesus points back to Moses, the Psalms, and the Prophets. Peter explains what David and Isaiah wrote. Paul identifies the promise to Abraham as the gospel announced beforehand.

They recognize a message that began before them, was fulfilled in Jesus Christ, and was entrusted to them to proclaim.

At its center is this good news: Christ died for our sins, was buried, and rose from the dead, according to the Scriptures. Forgiveness is proclaimed in His name to all nations.

Let’s begin with what Jesus says about the Scriptures. Then we’ll turn to the end of the Bible, hear His explanation of the earlier Scriptures, and return to Genesis to trace the message from its beginning.

**John 5:39**

> “You search the Scriptures, because you think that in them you have eternal life; and these are they which testify about me.”

Jesus identifies Himself as the One to whom the Scriptures bear witness. As we trace their message, we will follow that testimony concerning Him.

His next words also explain why this matters:

**John 5:40**

> “Yet you will not come to me, that you may have life.”

The Scriptures testify about Jesus so that we may come to Him for life. Studying them while refusing the One they reveal misses their testimony.

Now turn to the end of the Bible and listen to Jesus again.

**Revelation 22:16–17**

> “I, Jesus, have sent my angel to testify these things to you for the assemblies. I am the root and the offspring of David, the Bright and Morning Star.”
>
> The Spirit and the bride say, “Come!” He who hears, let him say, “Come!” He who is thirsty, let him come. He who desires, let him take the water of life freely.

Jesus identifies Himself as the root and offspring of David, connecting Him to the promises recorded in the earlier Scriptures. Here, at the end, the invitation is to receive the water of life freely.

How do those earlier Scriptures point to Him? After His resurrection, Jesus explains that connection to His disciples.

**Luke 24:27**

> Beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself.

**Luke 24:44**

> “All things which are written in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning me must be fulfilled.”

What was to be fulfilled?

**Luke 24:46–48**

> “Thus it is written, and thus it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead the third day, and that repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning at Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things.”

Jesus connects the earlier Scriptures to His suffering, resurrection, and the proclamation of forgiveness to all nations.

Let’s return to the beginning and consider why we need that forgiveness and life.

**Genesis 1:1**

> In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.

**Genesis 1:27**

> God created man in his own image. In God’s image he created him; male and female he created them.

**Genesis 1:31**

> God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good.

Scripture begins with God creating. Humanity belongs to Him, bears His image, and lives within a creation He declares very good.

**Genesis 2:7**

> Yahweh God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

God gives man life, places him in the garden, and speaks to him.

**Genesis 2:16–17**

> Yahweh God commanded the man, saying, “You may freely eat of every tree of the garden; but you shall not eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; for in the day that you eat of it, you will surely die.”

God’s generosity and His command appear together. Man may freely eat, but God identifies a forbidden tree and warns of death.

In the next chapter, another voice contradicts Him.

**Genesis 3:4–5**

> The serpent said to the woman, “You won’t really die, for God knows that in the day you eat it, your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.”

God says they will die. The serpent says they will not. These are conflicting claims about what is true and whether God can be trusted.

**Genesis 3:6**

> She took some of its fruit, and ate. Then she gave some to her husband with her, and he ate it, too.

They disobey. Later, God tells Adam:

**Genesis 3:19**

> “For you are dust, and you shall return to dust.”

The warning about death becomes part of the account of humanity’s future.

Paul picks up this account when explaining our need for Christ.

**Romans 5:12**

> Therefore, as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death passed to all men because all sinned.

Paul traces sin and death back to one man. He then explains what God accomplishes through Jesus Christ.

**Romans 5:17**

> For if by the trespass of the one, death reigned through the one; so much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one, Jesus Christ.

Death reigns through Adam’s trespass. Those who receive God’s grace and gift of righteousness reign in life through Jesus Christ.

Just before developing that connection, Paul writes:

**Romans 5:10–11**

> We were reconciled to God through the death of his Son…
>
> We also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation.

Paul’s explanation of salvation reaches back to Genesis. Christ’s death and the life He gives answer humanity’s need for reconciliation and deliverance from sin and death.

Now return to Genesis 3. Alongside the judgment, God announces what will happen to the serpent.

**Genesis 3:15**

> “I will put hostility between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring. He will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel.”

Genesis does not yet name Jesus. Read in light of the later Scriptures, Christians understand this as an early anticipation of the deliverer’s victory over the serpent.

As the account continues, God’s promise becomes more specific.

**Genesis 12:3**

God tells Abraham:

> “All the families of the earth will be blessed through you.”

**Genesis 22:18**

> “All the nations of the earth will be blessed by your offspring, because you have obeyed my voice.”

The promise extends to all nations.

**Galatians 3:8**

Paul explains:

> The Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the Good News beforehand to Abraham, saying, “In you all the nations will be blessed.”

Paul calls Abraham’s promise the Good News preached beforehand. He then identifies the promised offspring.

**Galatians 3:16**

> He doesn’t say, “To descendants”, as of many, but as of one, “To your offspring”, which is Christ.

Paul connects his gospel to the promise God gave Abraham.

**Deuteronomy 18:18–19**

God tells Moses:

> “I will raise them up a prophet from among their brothers, like you. I will put my words in his mouth, and he shall speak to them all that I shall command him. It shall happen, that whoever will not listen to my words which he shall speak in my name, I will require it of him.”

God promises a prophet who will speak His words. The people must listen.

**Acts 3:22, 25–26**

Peter quotes that promise while proclaiming Jesus:

> “The Lord God will raise up a prophet for you from among your brothers, like me. You shall listen to him in all things whatever he says to you.”

Then he recalls Abraham’s promise:

> “All the families of the earth will be blessed through your offspring.”

And identifies the One God sent:

> “God, having raised up his servant Jesus, sent him to you first to bless you, in turning away every one of you from your wickedness.”

Peter connects Moses, Abraham, and Jesus in one explanation of God’s promised blessing.

**Psalm 16:10**

David writes:

> For you will not leave my soul in Sheol, neither will you allow your holy one to see corruption.

What did David understand? Peter addresses that question directly.

**Acts 2:30–32**

> Being a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him that of the fruit of his body, according to the flesh, he would raise up the Christ to sit on his throne, he foreseeing this, spoke about the resurrection of the Christ, that his soul wasn’t left in Hades, and his flesh didn’t see decay.
>
> This Jesus God raised up, to which we all are witnesses.

David knew God’s promise and spoke ahead of Christ’s resurrection. Peter announces its fulfillment and gives the testimony of witnesses.

**Isaiah 52:13**

God announces:

> “Behold, my servant will deal wisely. He will be exalted and lifted up, and will be very high.”

Isaiah then describes the message concerning this servant.

**Isaiah 53:1**

> Who has believed our message? To whom has Yahweh’s arm been revealed?

The passage raises the question of belief. What is the message?

**Isaiah 53:5–6**

> But he was pierced for our transgressions.
> He was crushed for our iniquities.
> The punishment that brought our peace was on him;
> and by his wounds we are healed.
>
> All we like sheep have gone astray.
> Everyone has turned to his own way;
> and Yahweh has laid on him the iniquity of us all.

The servant bears the consequences of others’ sins.

**Isaiah 53:8–9**

> He was cut off out of the land of the living and stricken for the disobedience of my people…
>
> They made his grave with the wicked, and with a rich man in his death, although he had done no violence, nor was any deceit in his mouth.

He dies, although He is innocent. But Isaiah also describes the outcome of His work.

**Isaiah 53:10–12**

> When you make his soul an offering for sin, he will see his offspring. He will prolong his days and Yahweh’s pleasure will prosper in his hand.
>
> After the suffering of his soul, he will see the light and be satisfied. My righteous servant will justify many by the knowledge of himself; and he will bear their iniquities.
>
> Therefore I will give him a portion with the great. He will divide the plunder with the strong, because he poured out his soul to death and was counted with the transgressors; yet he bore the sins of many and made intercession for the transgressors.

God’s purpose succeeds through the servant. He bears sins, justifies many, and receives the reward God gives Him.

Who is Isaiah describing?

**Acts 8:34–35**

After reading from Isaiah 53, the Ethiopian official asks:

> “Who is the prophet talking about? About himself, or about someone else?”
>
> Philip opened his mouth, and beginning from this Scripture, preached to him about Jesus.

Philip begins with Isaiah and announces Jesus.

**Romans 10:16**

Paul also connects Isaiah’s message to the gospel:

> But they didn’t all listen to the glad news. For Isaiah says, “Lord, who has believed our report?”

Isaiah’s question about believing the report becomes Paul’s explanation of people’s response to the Good News.

**1 Peter 1:10–12**

Peter describes how the prophets understood their task:

> Concerning this salvation, the prophets sought and searched diligently. They prophesied of the grace that would come to you, searching for who or what kind of time the Spirit of Christ which was in them pointed to when he predicted the sufferings of Christ and the glories that would follow them.
>
> To them it was revealed that they served not themselves, but you, in these things, which now have been announced to you through those who preached the Good News to you by the Holy Spirit sent out from heaven; which things angels desire to look into.

The prophets searched to understand the fulfillment. They knew they were serving those who would come later. The things they anticipated were subsequently announced by gospel preachers through the Holy Spirit.

Peter connects the prophetic testimony and the gospel proclamation to the same salvation.

**Acts 26:22–23**

Paul describes his testimony this way:

> “Saying nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would happen, how the Christ must suffer, and how, by the resurrection of the dead, he would be first to proclaim light both to these people and to the Gentiles.”

Paul understands his proclamation as the fulfillment of what Moses and the prophets foretold. His explanation follows the pattern Jesus gave in Luke 24.

**1 Corinthians 15:3–4**

> For I delivered to you first of all that which I also received: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures.

Paul receives and delivers a message. Christ’s death for our sins and His resurrection are according to the Scriptures.

After naming witnesses to the resurrection, Paul says:

**1 Corinthians 15:11**

> Whether then it is I or they, so we preach, and so you believed.

Paul and the other apostles proclaim the same gospel. Their hearers receive the same testimony about Christ.

**2 Timothy 1:13–14**

Paul instructs Timothy:

> Hold the pattern of sound words which you have heard from me, in faith and love which is in Christ Jesus. That good thing which was committed to you, guard through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us.

Timothy must guard what he received.

**2 Timothy 2:2**

> The things which you have heard from me among many witnesses, commit the same things to faithful men who will be able to teach others also.

He must also pass it on. Paul teaches Timothy; Timothy entrusts the teaching to faithful people; they teach others.

What remains at its center?

**2 Timothy 2:8**

> Remember Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, of the offspring of David, according to my Good News.

The promised Christ, risen from the dead, remains the subject of the message being transmitted.

**Jude 3**

Jude urges his readers:

> “To contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints.”

They are responsible to preserve the faith already delivered to them.

**Romans 16:25–26**

In his closing doxology, Paul speaks of:

> “My Good News and the preaching of Jesus Christ…”

He connects that proclamation to what:

> “Now is revealed, and by the Scriptures of the prophets, according to the commandment of the eternal God, is made known for obedience of faith to all the nations.”

God’s revelation, the prophetic Scriptures, the proclamation of Jesus Christ, and the response of faith belong together.

We began by asking which voice has the authority to correct the others.

Scripture claims that God has spoken. We have heard its account of creation, God’s warning about death, and humanity’s disobedience. We have seen the prophets anticipate Christ, Jesus explain their testimony, and the apostles proclaim its fulfillment. They received a message they were responsible to preserve and pass on.

Their agreement matters, but agreement alone cannot make a message true. Their testimony asks us to consider the God who speaks, the promises He makes, and their fulfillment in Jesus Christ.

That message now reaches us. We are called to believe in the Christ it announces—the One who died for our sins and rose from the dead.

So the question becomes personal: When what God has revealed challenges what I have always believed, will I allow His Word to correct me? Will I trust His testimony about His Son?
