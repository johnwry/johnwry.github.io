# It Is Settled

## From God’s Perspective

*A Clear and Simple Tour of the Book of Romans*

> *After the suffering of his soul, he will see the light and be satisfied. ...*
>
> — Isaiah 53:11

**John Wry**
First edition
2026

Scripture quotations are from the World English Bible (WEB), Classic American English edition. Ellipses (...) indicate omitted text.

## Purpose of This Book

What can a person offer God after he has sinned?

A better life? A sincere promise? Years of service?

Those answers may seem reasonable until we consider what they must accomplish. They must answer for actual wrongdoing before a God who judges truthfully. Good intentions cannot make that wrongdoing innocent.

Romans brings us to that problem and then announces what God has done through Jesus Christ.

**“It is settled” means that God has dealt with the believer’s guilt through Christ, counted him righteous, and reconciled him to himself; the justification God gives is not a verdict the believer must earn again.**

The foundation is Christ’s death and resurrection. The one who justifies is God. Paul carries his explanation from sins forgiven and righteousness given to peace with God, salvation from wrath, and the future of those who belong to Christ.

This book follows that argument. It also addresses the commands and warnings that might seem to challenge its conclusion. They belong to the same letter and must be heard with the same care.

Paul wrote to people in Rome whom he called beloved of God. He wanted to visit them and was eager to proclaim the Good News among them. His letter speaks to Jew and Gentile alike, bringing both before God’s judgment and directing both to his provision through Christ.

We will follow the human need, God’s answer, and what follows from being justified. We will also consider his faithfulness to Israel and the life of service that proceeds from his mercies.

You need no theological training to begin, and you need not already agree. The question is what God has accomplished—and whether the answer leaves anything for us to add to the righteousness he provides.

## How to Use This Book

Read with Romans open beside you. Follow the thought from one movement to the next, and read the surrounding verses when a passage is quoted in part.

Bring your questions, including the ones that seem to have obvious answers. Familiar ideas can deserve another look. So can unfamiliar ones. A comforting conclusion needs grounds, and a frightening conclusion needs them too.

The title is a claim to examine. Scripture must supply its meaning and its proof.

# Prelude

## Who Defines Reality?

Before we ask whether it is settled with God, there is another question worth considering:

**Who gets to say?**

Who determines what is true about God, about us, and about the relationship between us? Is it what we have been taught? What we have experienced? What seems reasonable? What we feel most strongly?

We can carry answers to those questions without ever having put them into words. Perhaps we grew up with a particular understanding of God and have rarely examined it. Perhaps an experience changed what we believed about him. Perhaps we accept something when it makes sense to us and hesitate when it does not.

Why should the Bible settle the question? Before listening to its answer, we need to consider its claim to speak about God.

## Before We Had an Opinion

The Bible begins with a statement:

> In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.
>
> — Genesis 1:1

Within that account, human beings enter a world God has already made. He speaks, creates, names, and calls his creation good. We appear as his creatures, made in his image (Genesis 1:26–27).

We arrive in a world we did not make. We learn about it, question it, and sometimes misunderstand it. None of those activities makes us its creators.

Could the same distinction matter when we think about God?

If I misunderstand him, have I changed him? If I feel certain that he approves of me, does my certainty establish his approval? If I feel condemned, does that feeling tell me everything there is to know about his judgment?

A feeling tells us something about what we are experiencing. It cannot, on its own, tell us what God has decided.

The distinction becomes especially important when the question is personal: *What does God hold against me?*

I can describe my conduct. I can remember my failures. I can explain what I intended and how I feel about it now. But how will I know what God says about the matter?

## Has God Spoken?

Scripture presents God as someone who has made himself known.

> God, having in the past spoken to the fathers through the prophets at many times and in various ways, has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, through whom also he made the worlds.
>
> — Hebrews 1:1–2

God spoke through the prophets and through his Son. Scripture presents us with a God who has addressed us.

Paul makes the source and purpose of Scripture explicit when he writes to Timothy:

> Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness, that each person who belongs to God may be complete, thoroughly equipped for every good work.
>
> — 2 Timothy 3:16–17

Scripture claims to come from God and to correct those who read it. Listening to it means allowing an old assumption to be challenged.

That includes reassuring assumptions as well as frightening ones. An idea does not become true because it comforts us, and it does not become true because it is severe.

What happens, then, when Scripture challenges something that has always seemed obvious?

Suppose someone thinks, *God must accept me because I have tried so hard.* Another thinks, *God cannot accept me because I have failed so badly.* These are different conclusions, but each needs examination. What has God actually said about acceptance? What place does he give our works? What has he done about our sins?

Those are questions Scripture must answer.

## Bringing the Question to Romans

Paul tells Timothy that the holy Scriptures are able to make him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus (2 Timothy 3:15). We turn to Scripture to understand that salvation and the Christ through whom it comes.

Romans opens with Paul identifying himself as a servant of Jesus Christ, set apart for God’s Good News. He says that this message was promised through the prophets in the holy Scriptures and concerns God’s Son (Romans 1:1–4).

The message therefore asks us to consider more than our own assessment of our lives. It directs us to what God promised and what he has done through Jesus Christ.

What God says may challenge even our most familiar religious assumptions. Will we let him?

We do not need to pretend that we have no questions. We need to bring those questions to the text and give its answers careful attention.

“It is settled” concerns actual sins and the God to whom we must answer. The words will mean little unless Christ has accomplished what they claim.

Romans takes us to him.

# Movement 1 — The Good News of God

*Romans 1:1–17*

A greeting is easy to hurry past. Surely the important part comes later?

Paul has already begun. In naming the message entrusted to him, he places the whole letter before God.

> Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle, set apart for the Good News of God, which he promised before through his prophets in the holy Scriptures,
>
> — Romans 1:1–2

Paul introduces himself as someone who belongs to Jesus Christ and has been called to carry a message. He calls it the Good News of God. Its promise appears in Scripture before Paul’s ministry begins.

The message begins with God: his promise, his Son, and the servant he called to proclaim it.

Perhaps we have approached that question by considering what seems reasonable. Surely sincerity must count. Surely enough commitment, enough sacrifice, or enough improvement must make a difference.

But how would we know what is enough? And who would have the authority to decide?

Paul’s opening gives us somewhere to begin beyond our own estimates. Writing to the Galatians, he explains the source of his message:

> But I make known to you, brothers, concerning the Good News which was preached by me, that it is not according to man. For I didn’t receive it from man, nor was I taught it, but it came to me through revelation of Jesus Christ.
>
> — Galatians 1:11–12

Paul traces the message to Jesus Christ. Its content must come before our guesses about what God would say.

## Concerning His Son

What is this Good News about?

Paul continues:

> concerning his Son, who was born of the offspring of David according to the flesh, who was declared to be the Son of God with power according to the Spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord,
>
> — Romans 1:3–4

The message concerns God’s Son. Paul speaks of his descent from David, his resurrection from the dead, and his identity as Jesus Christ our Lord.

The Son came from David’s line and rose from the dead. What he accomplished will explain how God receives those who believe in him.

It is possible to take Christianity seriously while giving most of our attention to something else: what we have promised, attempted, sacrificed, or failed to do.

I know something about that.

I served as a missionary for thirteen years. I was sincere. I wanted to help people, and I wanted to do something for God. I made commitments, promised to do better, and worked at keeping those promises. In many respects, I was disciplined.

Sometimes I felt proud of reaching a spiritual goal. At other times, I felt guilty and condemned because I had failed to meet my own expectations. I lived through repeated cycles of effort, achievement, pride, failure, and guilt.

By the time someone asked me a particular question, I was deeply tired—spiritually and ministerially exhausted.

He asked, “John, if you died today and God asked you, ‘Why should I let you in?’ what would you answer?”

My answer came quickly.

“Because I gave you my life.”

He replied, “How interesting. I thought He gave His life for you.”

That answer disarmed me. Asked why God should receive me, I had pointed to what I had given him.

I was not denying that Jesus had given his life. I preached that message. Yet when the question became personal, my answer was about my contribution. My service had become the reason I offered for being accepted.

The conversation exposed a question I needed to examine: **What was I actually relying on when I thought about my standing before God?**

The conversation exposed my confusion. To answer it, I needed more than another conversation about my service. I needed to understand what Christ had done.

That is where Paul begins: the Good News concerns God’s Son.

## Why Preach to Believers?

Paul addressed his readers as beloved of God and called to be saints. He thanked God for their faith and wanted to visit so that they could be encouraged by one another’s faith (Romans 1:7–12).

Then he wrote:

> So as much as is in me, I am eager to preach the Good News to you also who are in Rome.
>
> — Romans 1:15

They already believed, and Paul still wanted to preach the Good News to them.

Familiar words can conceal an old misunderstanding. I knew the language of Christ’s sacrifice, yet answered a question about acceptance by naming my own.

What would we point to if asked why God should receive us? The answer reveals what we think acceptance rests on.

Nor does examining those grounds make service unimportant. Paul himself was a servant. He says that through Jesus Christ he received grace and apostleship for the obedience of faith among the nations (Romans 1:5). His work belongs within what he received through Christ.

Service has a place in Paul’s life. Whether it can justify him before God is another question.

## God’s Power for Salvation

Paul explains why he is eager to preach:

> For I am not ashamed of the Good News of Christ, because it is the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes, for the Jew first, and also for the Greek.
>
> — Romans 1:16

Paul identifies the power as God’s and its purpose as salvation. He also identifies those to whom it applies: everyone who believes, both Jew and Greek.

The power to save belongs to God. The person believes the message concerning his Son. Paul holds those together without making faith the source of God’s power.

This gives direction to the question my answer had raised. To understand why God receives a person, we must examine his saving action through Christ. An account of our effort cannot take the place of that explanation.

Paul continues:

> For in it is revealed God’s righteousness from faith to faith. As it is written, “But the righteous shall live by faith.”
>
> — Romans 1:17

God’s righteousness is revealed in the Good News. Paul introduces here a subject he will develop throughout the argument that follows.

How does God deal righteously with people who have sinned? How can he justify them? What does faith have to do with it?

Paul will answer by showing both the guilt of the sinner and the righteousness of God in dealing with that guilt.

My answer had begun, “Because I gave you my life.” Paul’s message requires me to consider what God has done through Jesus Christ.

Before the answer can be understood, the need for it must come into view.

Paul turns next to the ungodliness and unrighteousness against which God’s wrath is revealed. To understand what has been settled, we must understand the matter that needed settling.

# Movement 2 — The Truth Exchanged

*Romans 1:18–32*

What happens when we know something is true but do not want to live with what it means?

We might avoid it, find reasons to dismiss it, or surround ourselves with people who agree that it does not matter. The truth itself has not changed. Our response to it has.

Paul brings us to a far more serious instance of that problem. Having introduced the Good News as God’s power for salvation, he begins to explain why salvation is needed.

> For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men who suppress the truth in unrighteousness, because that which is known of God is revealed in them, for God revealed it to them.
>
> — Romans 1:18–19

The language is direct. There is truth that God has made known, there are people suppressing it, and there is God’s wrath against their ungodliness and unrighteousness.

This takes the discussion beyond whether life is working well or whether someone feels satisfied. Paul is describing a problem before God. His judgment belongs to the explanation from the beginning.

If we are going to understand what Christ has settled, we must understand the seriousness of what stands against us.

## What Has Been Made Known?

Paul explains why he holds these people accountable:

> For the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made, even his everlasting power and divinity, that they may be without excuse.
>
> — Romans 1:20

The things God made disclose something about their Maker. Paul specifically names his everlasting power and divinity. His conclusion is that those he describes are without excuse.

Creation makes God’s power and divinity known. Paul holds people responsible for their response to what God has revealed.

> Because knowing God, they didn’t glorify him as God, and didn’t give thanks, but became vain in their reasoning, and their senseless heart was darkened.
>
> — Romans 1:21

Surely the trouble begins with the worst things people do?

Paul begins with honor and gratitude withheld from God. Before describing the conduct that follows, he brings us to the Creator whom people refuse to honor.

What does it mean to recognize a Creator while withholding the honor due to him? Can we acknowledge that God exists and still treat him as though he has no claim on us?

Paul’s words reach beneath a merely verbal acknowledgment. The response he describes concerns how people regard the God they know. Their reasoning becomes futile, and their hearts are darkened. They profess wisdom, yet become fools.

The problem already involves thought, worship, and conduct together.

## Something Takes His Place

Paul then describes an exchange:

> and traded the glory of the incorruptible God for the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds, four-footed animals, and creeping things.
>
> — Romans 1:23

They trade the glory of the incorruptible God for images of corruptible creatures. The contrast is between the Creator and what he has made.

A few verses later, Paul states it again:

> who exchanged the truth of God for a lie, and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever. Amen.
>
> — Romans 1:25

This helps us understand what it means to displace God. No one has removed him from existence or taken away his authority. People have directed their worship and service elsewhere. Something created receives what belongs to the Creator.

God has not been removed from existence. Worship and service have been given to something he made. The altar is occupied; the Creator has been refused his place.

Religious activity continues. That alone proves little. Worship directed toward a creature is still a refusal to honor the Creator.

## God Gives Them Up

Three times in this passage, Paul says that God gave them up.

The first follows the exchange of God’s glory for images:

> Therefore God also gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to uncleanness, that their bodies should be dishonored among themselves;
>
> — Romans 1:24

God answers the exchange by giving them up to uncleanness and bodily dishonor. Their ability to pursue what they want is no proof of his approval. Paul began this account with God’s wrath.

Paul repeats the statement in verse 26:

> For this reason, God gave them up to vile passions. ...
>
> — Romans 1:26

He describes women exchanging the natural sexual function for what is against nature and men leaving the natural function of the woman, desiring one another and committing what he calls inappropriate acts (Romans 1:26–27).

The account continues. The corruption reaches beyond these acts into a wider range of wrongdoing.

The third statement concerns the mind:

> Even as they refused to have God in their knowledge, God gave them up to a reprobate mind, to do those things which are not fitting;
>
> — Romans 1:28

Here again, Paul connects their refusal with God’s action. They refuse to retain God in their knowledge, and he gives them up to a corrupt mind and unfitting conduct.

The repeated words carry the account downward: God gave them up. Bodies are dishonored, passions are degraded, and the mind is given over to what is unfit. Sin reaches through the person, and God’s judgment is already at work.

## A List That Comes Close to Home

Paul’s description includes sexual immorality, wickedness, greed, envy, murder, strife, deceit, gossip, slander, arrogance, boasting, disobedience to parents, faithlessness, and lack of mercy (Romans 1:29–31).

It is difficult to keep that list at a comfortable distance.

We might readily condemn murder while excusing our own envy. We might be disturbed by sexual wrongdoing while treating gossip as harmless conversation. We might object to someone else’s arrogance without examining what we boast about.

Paul places these things within the same account of unrighteousness. Their inclusion prevents us from reducing the passage to the sins that happen to offend us most.

Nor does he treat conduct as an unimportant symptom. The acts themselves are wrong. People are harmed, God is dishonored, and those who practice such things are accountable to him.

Paul closes the description with another reference to knowledge:

> who, knowing the ordinance of God, that those who practice such things are worthy of death, not only do the same, but also approve of those who practice them.
>
> — Romans 1:32

They know God’s judgment, yet practice these things and approve of others doing them.

Approval does not change the judgment. Agreement among people does not make their conduct right before God. The passage began with suppressed truth and ends with wrongdoing practiced and endorsed despite knowledge of its seriousness.

## The Problem Before God

God has made himself known and has been refused. His truth has been exchanged for a lie. The corruption that follows does not place humanity beyond his reach; it belongs within the account of his judgment.

A better opinion of ourselves cannot answer that judgment. Neither can the approval of people who practice the same things. The need is becoming clear: actual wrongdoing stands before a God who judges it.

This is why the question of salvation matters. If something is to be settled, it must be settled before the God whose truth has been suppressed and whose judgment has been named.

Perhaps we agree with every word. We can see the disorder and name those responsible.

Surely that puts us on the right side of the matter?

Paul’s next words address the person who judges.

# Movement 3 — No Exemption

*Romans 2:1–3:20*

It is possible to agree with everything Paul has said so far and still miss its relevance to ourselves.

We can recognize dishonesty, cruelty, arrogance, and corruption. We can name people who practice them. We may even have suffered because of their actions.

But recognizing another person’s guilt does not establish our innocence.

Paul now turns directly to the person who judges:

> Therefore you are without excuse, O man, whoever you are who judge. For in that which you judge another, you condemn yourself. For you who judge practice the same things.
>
> — Romans 2:1

His accusation is specific. The person condemning others practices the same things. The judgment he pronounces therefore reaches his own conduct.

Paul is not saying that every moral distinction is wrong. He is exposing the contradiction of condemning an action in someone else while expecting exemption for ourselves.

Can we recognize that contradiction in ordinary life? We object when someone deceives us, yet find explanations for our own dishonesty. We resent being treated with contempt, yet excuse the contempt in our own words. The circumstances may differ, but do those differences always justify the different verdicts we give?

Paul brings the question before God:

> Do you think this, O man who judges those who practice such things, and do the same, that you will escape the judgment of God?
>
> — Romans 2:3

Being able to identify wrongdoing does not place us beyond judgment. God’s judgment is according to truth (Romans 2:2). It includes what we do, not merely what we condemn.

## Patience Is Not Approval

There is another assumption Paul challenges. If judgment has not fallen, perhaps we take its absence as evidence that all is well.

Paul gives God’s patience a different meaning:

> Or do you despise the riches of his goodness, forbearance, and patience, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?
>
> — Romans 2:4

God’s goodness calls for repentance. It gives the person Paul addresses reason to turn from wrongdoing.

The next verse describes someone who remains hard and unrepentant, accumulating wrath for the day of God’s righteous judgment (Romans 2:5). Continued patience does not mean that the wrongdoing has become acceptable or that accountability has disappeared.

This matters to the question of what is settled. We cannot establish peace with God merely by observing that life has continued and no immediate judgment has arrived. Paul directs us to ask what God’s patience means and how we are responding to it.

## God Shows No Partiality

Paul then describes God as the one who repays each person according to their works. He speaks of eternal life for those who persevere in doing good and seek glory, honor, and incorruptibility, and of wrath for those who are self-seeking and obey unrighteousness rather than truth (Romans 2:6–8).

He applies this to both Jew and Greek, then states the reason:

> For there is no partiality with God.
>
> — Romans 2:11

God’s judgment distinguishes good from evil. No religious name or ancestry gives someone permission to disobey.

That raises a question we cannot skip: if God judges according to works, how will Paul arrive at justification apart from works of the law?

For now, the judging person has lost his exemption. God’s standard reaches him too.

His next distinction concerns possessing the law:

> For it isn’t the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of the law will be justified
>
> — Romans 2:13

Hearing what God requires is not the same as doing it. Access to the law does not make someone righteous merely because they know its words.

Nor does being without the law remove accountability. Paul speaks of Gentiles who do things the law requires, showing its work written in their hearts, with conscience and thoughts accusing or excusing them. He brings the discussion to the day when God judges people’s secrets through Jesus Christ (Romans 2:12–16).

The judgment reaches further than public reputation. Neither possession of the law nor its absence supplies an exemption.

## When Knowledge Becomes a Credential

Paul now addresses the person who bears the name of a Jew, rests on the law, knows God’s will, and is confident of being able to instruct others (Romans 2:17–20).

These are real privileges. Knowing what God has said matters. Being able to teach it can benefit others.

But Paul asks whether the teacher’s conduct agrees with the teaching:

> You therefore who teach another, don’t you teach yourself? You who preach that a man shouldn’t steal, do you steal?
>
> — Romans 2:21

He continues with adultery, sacrilege, and the dishonor brought to God through disobedience. The issue is not whether the person can explain the command. It is whether the person obeys it.

There is a question here for anyone familiar with Scripture. What do we think our knowledge establishes about us?

Knowing the right answer can make us useful in a discussion. It does not make an act of disobedience disappear. Teaching someone else to be honest does not answer for our own deceit. Familiarity with God’s words cannot be substituted for doing what they say.

Paul makes the same point concerning circumcision. He acknowledges its value, then says that breaking the law undermines the claim someone might make on the basis of that outward sign. He concludes by speaking of what is inward, of circumcision of the heart, and of praise that comes from God rather than people (Romans 2:25–29).

The outward sign does not give its bearer permission to disregard the God to whom it points.

Has receiving God’s law, then, been worth nothing? Paul answers:

> Then what advantage does the Jew have? Or what is the profit of circumcision? Much in every way! Because first of all, they were entrusted with the revelations of God.
>
> — Romans 3:1–2

His answer prevents us from saying that receiving God’s words has no value. It is a privilege to be entrusted with them.

But privilege and innocence are different matters. Possessing God’s revelation does not remove the need to answer for disobedience.

Paul also refuses to let human unfaithfulness become an accusation against God. The failure of some to believe does not nullify God’s faithfulness. God remains true (Romans 3:3–4).

Then he addresses another possible escape: if human unrighteousness makes God’s righteousness evident, would it be unjust for God to judge it?

Paul rejects that reasoning. God’s righteousness does not turn our wrongdoing into something deserving approval. Nor does good resulting from evil make the evil right (Romans 3:5–8).

The excuses are being examined one by one. Condemning others does not establish innocence. Knowing the law does not equal keeping it. A religious privilege does not cancel disobedience. God’s faithfulness does not make human unfaithfulness harmless.

## All Under Sin

Paul now gathers the argument:

> What then? Are we better than they? No, in no way. For we previously warned both Jews and Greeks that they are all under sin.
>
> — Romans 3:9

Jew and Gentile have received different things. Both stand under the charge of sin.

Paul supports that charge with Scripture. He describes the absence of righteousness, understanding, and seeking God. He names deceitful speech, bitterness, violence, destruction, and the absence of the fear of God (Romans 3:10–18).

The accusation concerns people in their thinking, speaking, acting, and relationship to God. It does not require that everyone has committed every sin in the same way or to the same degree. Paul’s stated conclusion is that both Jews and Greeks are under sin.

We may differ greatly from the person beside us. That difference does not, by itself, answer the charge against us.

This is where comparison fails as a defense. Finding someone who has acted worse does not explain why our own wrongdoing should be counted as righteousness.

## Every Mouth Closed

Paul concludes:

> Now we know that whatever things the law says, it speaks to those who are under the law, that every mouth may be closed, and all the world may be brought under the judgment of God.
>
> — Romans 3:19

The law speaks even to those who might have claimed its possession as their defense. They do not stand outside its judgment.

Every mouth closed is a striking picture. We have heard the judging person, the knowledgeable person, and the person appealing to religious privilege. None of those claims has removed the guilt Paul identifies.

He then states the conclusion plainly:

> Because by the works of the law, no flesh will be justified in his sight; for through the law comes the knowledge of sin.
>
> — Romans 3:20

The statement about the doers of the law has not been withdrawn. God does not count hearing as obedience. But who now stands before that standard? Paul has charged Jew and Gentile alike with sin. The law exposes their disobedience, and he expressly concludes that its works will justify no one in God’s sight.

Romans 2:13 gives the hearer no shelter. Romans 3:20 closes the attempt to claim righteousness through his record. The guilty person needs justification that his works cannot provide.

The law’s judgment is right. That is precisely the trouble for the person offering his record as a defense: the same law exposes the sin in it.

There must be something left to offer—better intentions, greater effort, a stronger commitment. But which of those turns the guilt already exposed into righteousness?

His conclusion concerns what happens **in God’s sight**. The issue has never been limited to whether we can reassure ourselves or persuade someone else to approve of us.

We stand accountable before God, and works of the law do not justify us before him.

That is where this part of the argument leaves us. It is also where Paul turns to explain the righteousness God has revealed through faith in Jesus Christ.

The next movement begins with his words: **“But now ...”**

# Movement 4 — God Acts

*Romans 3:21–31*

Paul has brought us to a conclusion we cannot improve by comparison: both Jews and Greeks are under sin. The law exposes that sin, and works of the law do not justify us before God.

What answer remains for someone who stands guilty?

Paul continues:

> But now apart from the law, a righteousness of God has been revealed, being testified by the law and the prophets;
>
> — Romans 3:21

The law has left the sinner without a defense. Now Paul announces righteousness from God, revealed apart from the law.

Yet this does not make the message a contradiction of what God previously said. The law and the prophets testify to it. The Scriptures that expose the problem also bear witness to God’s answer.

Paul identifies how this righteousness reaches those who need it:

> even the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ to all and on all those who believe. For there is no distinction, for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God;
>
> — Romans 3:22–23

The absence of distinction continues from the previous movement. All have sinned. Neither Jew nor Gentile can claim exemption from that need. And the righteousness Paul announces is through faith in Jesus Christ, for those who believe.

He is not describing a second opportunity to establish our own innocence. He is explaining what God provides for sinners.

## Justified Freely

Paul brings three important terms together:

> being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,
>
> — Romans 3:24

To be *justified* is to be counted righteous before God. The person who could offer no adequate defense is given a righteous standing through Christ.

God gives that verdict. It is not a new description the sinner invents for himself.

Then comes *freely by his grace*. The justification is given. Paul has not introduced a new payment the sinner must make or a better record the sinner must first produce.

Finally, he names its basis: *the redemption that is in Christ Jesus*.

Grace does not mean that nothing had to be done about sin. The gift comes through Christ’s redemption. Elsewhere, Paul connects that redemption directly with forgiveness:

> In him we have our redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace
>
> — Ephesians 1:7

The language concerns release from the sins that stand against us. Paul connects it with Christ’s blood and God’s grace. The person receiving forgiveness does not earn it, but the forgiveness is grounded in what Christ has done.

The gift is free to the recipient. Its cost brings us to Christ’s blood.

## God Deals with Sin

Paul describes Christ as the one:

> whom God sent to be an atoning sacrifice through faith in his blood, for a demonstration of his righteousness through the passing over of prior sins, in God’s forbearance;
>
> — Romans 3:25

The blood and the sacrifice keep us from treating justification as a decision to overlook the seriousness of sin.

What does an atoning sacrifice accomplish? Scripture connects Christ’s sacrifice with dealing with sin itself. Hebrews says:

> ... But now once at the end of the ages, he has been revealed to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself.
>
> — Hebrews 9:26

Christ offered himself. The sacrifice addresses the sin that made justification necessary.

God sent Christ to be this sacrifice. The one before whom sinners stand guilty has provided the answer to their guilt.

Paul also explains why this demonstrates God’s righteousness. He refers to prior sins passed over in God’s forbearance. Earlier, we saw that patience must not be mistaken for approval. Here Paul shows that God’s forbearance does not mean he has abandoned righteousness.

Sin has not become acceptable. God’s answer to it is displayed in Christ.

Paul continues:

> to demonstrate his righteousness at this present time, that he might himself be just and the justifier of him who has faith in Jesus.
>
> — Romans 3:26

God remains just as he justifies the person who has faith in Jesus. Acceptance has not been obtained by persuading him to lower his standard.

The accusation was real. The sinner’s inability to establish righteousness through works of the law was real. God’s provision in Christ is also real, and Paul presents it as the demonstration of his righteousness.

When we say that Christ is sufficient, this is part of what we mean. God himself presents Christ’s sacrifice as the basis on which he righteously justifies the believer. We are not being asked to add enough personal worth to make that action defensible.

## What Faith Receives

Where does faith belong in this?

Paul speaks of faith in Jesus Christ and in his blood. The sinner believes in the one God has provided.

Imagine someone saying, “I understand that my good works cannot justify me. Perhaps the strength of my believing can.”

The attention has returned to something about the person: whether their faith is impressive enough to deserve the verdict.

Paul’s explanation directs us to Jesus Christ and his blood. The saving provision is there. Faith is directed toward the one God has provided; it does not supply another sacrifice or give God a second reason to justify.

Paul brings this to a question about boasting.

## What Is Left to Boast About?

> Where then is the boasting? It is excluded. By what kind of law? Of works? No, but by a law of faith.
>
> — Romans 3:27

If justification came through our achievement, we could point to that achievement as the reason for our standing. But Paul excludes boasting.

The sinner does not provide the redemption. The sinner does not become the atoning sacrifice. God provides Christ and justifies the person who believes.

Paul gathers the conclusion:

> We maintain therefore that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law.
>
> — Romans 3:28

The sinner who could not be justified by works is justified through faith in Jesus Christ. Redemption is found in him.

My missionary answer had been, “Because I gave you my life.” It offered my contribution as the reason God should receive me. Paul’s explanation gives me a different answer to examine: what God has provided through Christ.

This is not a dismissal of service. It identifies what service cannot supply. It cannot become the grounds of a justification Paul describes as freely given by grace.

Paul then asks whether God belongs to Jews only. His answer includes Gentiles, because there is one God who justifies the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith (Romans 3:29–30).

There is no separate arrangement by which one group earns what another receives as a gift. The distinction that could not remove anyone’s guilt does not create two ways of justification.

Paul closes with another question:

> Do we then nullify the law through faith? May it never be! No, we establish the law.
>
> — Romans 3:31

Justification by faith does not require us to declare the law false or worthless. Paul has allowed its testimony to stand: it exposes sin, and it bears witness to the righteousness now revealed. The answer in Christ does not depend on denying what the law has said.

God has answered sin through Christ’s sacrifice. He remains righteous, and he freely justifies the believer. The sinner’s works have supplied no part of the redemption.

The question is no longer whether we can produce enough righteousness to persuade him. Paul has explained how God justifies someone who cannot present that defense.

Now he turns to Scripture to show that this is no departure from God’s dealings with those who came before.

# Movement 4, Continued — What God Counts

*Romans 4:1–25*

If justification is by faith apart from works of the law, what should we make of Abraham?

Paul brings him into the argument directly. If Abraham was justified by works, he would have something to boast about—but not before God (Romans 4:1–2).

Then Paul asks the question that governs the discussion:

> For what does the Scripture say? “Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.”
>
> — Romans 4:3

The statement identifies both Abraham’s response and what was counted to him. He believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.

Paul explains the significance by distinguishing a wage from a gift.

## Something Owed or Something Given?

> Now to him who works, the reward is not counted as grace, but as something owed. But to him who doesn’t work, but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is accounted for righteousness.
>
> — Romans 4:4–5

A wage is owed for work done. Paying it is the fulfillment of an obligation.

Paul places justification in a different category. He describes someone who does not work but believes in the God who justifies the ungodly. Faith is accounted for righteousness.

Surely God must wait until someone becomes godly before declaring him righteous?

Paul names the person God justifies: the ungodly. The sinner has not first become worthy. He believes in the God who counts righteousness apart from works—the God whose righteous answer to sin Paul has just explained through Christ’s sacrifice.

The question is how the sinner is justified. Work offered as a claim upon God would make acceptance a wage. Paul places it under grace.

That distinction reaches the heart of performance as a means of acceptance. If I think God owes me a favorable verdict because of what I have done, I am describing a wage. Paul is describing grace.

## Sins Not Charged

Paul then brings David into the discussion:

> Even as David also pronounces blessing on the man to whom God counts righteousness apart from works:
>
> — Romans 4:6

He quotes David’s description of that blessing:

> “Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man whom the Lord will by no means charge with sin.”
>
> — Romans 4:7–8

David’s blessing belongs to someone with sins to forgive. The wrong was done, yet the Lord will not charge him with it. Paul places that forgiveness beside righteousness counted apart from works.

Paul places this beside the counting of righteousness apart from works. The answer to guilt is not a more favorable comparison with another person. It is God’s forgiveness and his accounting of righteousness.

God has not mistaken the record. Christ’s redemption explains how he deals righteously with what the record contains.

The blessing rests on what God does about actual sin.

Does this blessing depend on circumcision?

Paul answers by examining the order in Abraham’s case. Was righteousness accounted to him before or after he was circumcised?

Before (Romans 4:9–10).

That order matters. Paul calls circumcision a sign and a seal of the righteousness of the faith Abraham already had while uncircumcised. The sign did not produce the righteousness it sealed (Romans 4:11).

Abraham therefore stands as the father of those who believe while uncircumcised, as well as of the circumcised who walk in the steps of his faith (Romans 4:11–12).

Paul is continuing the argument about Jew and Gentile. Neither ancestry nor the outward sign can replace faith. The blessing is not confined to those who bear the sign Abraham later received.

## Why the Promise Is Sure

Paul then turns to the promise. He says it came to Abraham and his offspring through the righteousness of faith, rather than through the law. If inheritance depended on the law, faith would be made void and the promise ineffective; the law produces wrath (Romans 4:13–15).

Then he states the connection between faith, grace, and certainty:

> For this cause it is of faith, that it may be according to grace, to the end that the promise may be sure to all the offspring, not to that only which is of the law, but to that also which is of the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all.
>
> — Romans 4:16

Paul does not treat grace as a weakness in the arrangement. He connects it with the promise being sure.

The promise is not secured by turning it into a reward owed under the law. It is of faith, according to grace, and extends to those who share Abraham’s faith.

Grace makes the promise sure. Its fulfillment rests on the power of the God who made it.

## The God Abraham Believed

What did Abraham’s faith involve?

Paul describes God as the one who gives life to the dead and calls things that are not as though they were. Abraham believed the promise that he would become the father of many nations, despite the condition of his own aged body and Sarah’s womb (Romans 4:17–19).

Paul does not describe faith as empty optimism. It has something specific to rely on: God’s promise and his ability to fulfill it.

> Yet, looking to the promise of God, he didn’t waver through unbelief, but grew strong through faith, giving glory to God, and being fully assured that what he had promised, he was also able to perform.
>
> — Romans 4:20–21

Abraham’s confidence concerned God’s ability to do what God had promised.

That is more specific than telling ourselves that everything will work out. Faith has an object and a stated promise. Abraham relied on God to accomplish what the circumstances gave him no reason to expect from his own resources.

Paul takes Abraham’s believing seriously. He describes its strength and assurance. Yet the ability to fulfill the promise belongs to God.

The distinction keeps us from treating faith as the power that performs God’s work. Abraham believed the one who could perform it.

## Written for Us Also

Paul does not leave Abraham’s experience in the past.

> Now it was not written that it was accounted to him for his sake alone, but for our sake also, to whom it will be accounted, who believe in him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead, who was delivered up for our trespasses, and was raised for our justification.
>
> — Romans 4:23–25

Here the argument comes directly to us.

Paul identifies those to whom righteousness will be accounted: those who believe in the God who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead. He identifies what Jesus’ death concerned—our trespasses—and connects his resurrection with our justification.

The message is grounded in what God has done. Our sins are named, Christ’s death addresses them, and his resurrection belongs to the explanation of our justification.

The guilt has been answered through Christ. Righteousness is counted, sins are forgiven, and the promise is made sure according to grace. Each part of the answer directs us to what God gives.

We do not present our service as an unpaid invoice and ask God to settle it with acceptance. We believe in the God who justifies the ungodly, who raised Jesus from the dead, and who accounts righteousness apart from works.

Paul has explained the grounds of justification. He now turns to what follows from being justified.

# Movement 5 — Peace with God

*Romans 5:1–11*

What changes when God justifies someone?

Paul has explained that justification is given freely by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus. He has shown from Abraham and David that God counts righteousness apart from works. Now he describes what follows:

> Being therefore justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ; through whom we also have our access by faith into this grace in which we stand. We rejoice in hope of the glory of God.
>
> — Romans 5:1–2

Peace with God follows justification. It is not a feeling they must first achieve to become acceptable.

God has justified them. What has changed between them and him?

## Peace with Whom?

We use the word *peace* in several ways. We might mean a quiet afternoon, an agreement after an argument, or relief from anxiety.

Paul specifies the relationship: peace **with God**, through our Lord Jesus Christ.

Later in this passage, he describes our former condition as being enemies and our present condition as being reconciled. That helps explain the peace he announces. Something has changed between us and God through Christ.

The question is therefore larger than whether we feel peaceful.

Suppose someone says, “I know what Romans says, but I still feel as though God is against me.” That feeling deserves an honest hearing. Yet the feeling and Paul’s statement concern different things. One describes the person’s experience; the other describes the relationship established through justification.

Paul locates the peace in justification through Jesus Christ. A troubled person’s feelings do not supply its foundation or erase the foundation he names.

## The Grace in Which We Stand

Paul also says that through Christ we have access by faith into the grace in which we stand.

Grace is not merely something mentioned at the beginning and then left behind. Paul describes it as our present standing. The same Christ through whom we have peace is the one through whom we have access.

Perhaps you have thought of acceptance as a running balance. Obedience adds something; failure subtracts something. At the end of the day, you try to determine whether enough remains for God to receive you.

Paul has already distinguished grace from a wage owed for work. Here he describes justified people standing in grace. He directs them to Christ as the one through whom they have access.

Paul will carry this standing forward into an explanation of their future. First, he speaks of something already present in their lives: suffering.

## Suffering Within the Relationship

> Not only this, but we also rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces perseverance; and perseverance, proven character; and proven character, hope;
>
> — Romans 5:3–4

Paul does not describe peace with God as an escape from every painful circumstance. Suffering remains within the lives of those he has just described as justified.

The people at peace with God are the people who suffer. Hardship alone cannot prove that the relationship has broken.

He also describes a progression: suffering produces perseverance, perseverance proven character, and proven character hope. He is explaining why hope can remain in suffering, rather than making the absence of suffering its foundation.

Then he gives the reason this hope does not disappoint:

> and hope doesn’t disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us.
>
> — Romans 5:5

God has given the Holy Spirit and poured his love into our hearts. Paul’s explanation includes what God gives within us.

But he does not leave the meaning of God’s love dependent on our ability to describe an inward experience. His next words point to what Christ did and to the condition we were in when he did it.

## When Did God Love Us?

> For while we were yet weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly.
>
> — Romans 5:6

Weak and ungodly. Those are Paul’s descriptions.

God did not wait for the people in this account to establish their worthiness before Christ died for them. Their need was present when he acted.

Paul then considers the unusual willingness of one person to die for another. Someone might perhaps dare to die for a good person. Against that possibility, he places God’s action:

> But God commends his own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.
>
> — Romans 5:8

The demonstration of love occurred while we were sinners.

This answers an important question about its source. God’s love was not first brought into existence by our improvement. Christ died for us in the condition from which we needed rescue.

God acted while they were sinners. What, then, will he do for those he has now justified?

## Much More

> Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we will be saved from God’s wrath through him.
>
> — Romans 5:9

Christ died for sinners. Those now justified by his blood will be saved from wrath through him. Paul carries the same people from the completed action into the promised future.

He does not move from Christ’s death to uncertainty about whether it will prove sufficient. He moves from what has happened to what will follow.

The words *much more* express the strength of that connection. The God who acted for us in our sinful condition has now justified us through Christ’s blood. Paul gives that present justification as a reason for the future salvation he announces.

God’s action has established more than a promising beginning. Present justification leads, in Paul’s argument, to future salvation through the same Christ.

## From Enemies to Reconciled

Paul states the reasoning again, this time using the language of reconciliation:

> For if while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we will be saved by his life.
>
> — Romans 5:10

Enemies were reconciled through the Son’s death. Now reconciled, they will be saved by his life.

Reconciliation concerns the relationship that was broken. Paul does not describe people persuading God to overlook their hostility by eventually becoming useful to him. Reconciliation came through the death of his Son while they were enemies.

What, then, should we conclude about those who have been reconciled?

Paul’s answer is *much more*. If reconciliation came through Christ’s death while we were enemies, salvation follows through his life now that we are reconciled.

The risen Christ belongs to the answer as fully as the Christ who died. Paul has already spoken of Jesus being raised for our justification. Here he speaks of salvation by his life.

The argument does not end with our past sins being addressed and our future being handed over to our ability to make ourselves acceptable. Paul continues to identify Christ as the one through whom we will be saved.

The reason lies in the Son himself: his death reconciled enemies, and his life secures the salvation Paul announces for those reconciled.

Paul closes this part of the argument with a present fact:

> Not only so, but we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation.
>
> — Romans 5:11

We have **now received** reconciliation.

Paul does not leave it as a possibility awaiting enough years of satisfactory performance. He describes something received through Jesus Christ.

There is still a future salvation in this passage. Paul has not said that every part of God’s saving purpose has already occurred. But he gives those justified and reconciled a stated reason for expecting that future: the Christ through whom God has already acted for them.

Peace, access, and reconciliation have been given through Christ. Their future reaches beyond the present because the one who died for them lives.

The matter is not held open until we can supplement his blood with sufficient worthiness of our own. The blood by which we are justified is his. The death through which we are reconciled is his. The life by which Paul says we will be saved is his.

Our standing before God rests on that provision.

Having established these consequences of justification, Paul now widens the argument. He will consider the sin and death that came through Adam alongside the grace and life that come through Jesus Christ.

# Movement 6 — Sin Is No Longer the Master

*Romans 5:12–6:23*

Christ died for sinners. Through his death, God reconciled enemies to himself. Paul has now carried that argument forward: those reconciled through Christ’s death will be saved by his life.

But what about sin? It remains present, and its demands can be strong. How does that fit with what God has accomplished?

For years, I struggled with sins hidden from the people I ministered to. My attention stayed on the struggle itself. I would fail, promise to do better, and begin again. Sometimes there was progress. Then another failure would bring me back to the same question: why couldn’t I overcome this?

Much of my life became an effort to manage sin.

Paul approaches the matter by explaining something I had not adequately considered: the relationship to sin has changed through Christ. To understand that change, we need to go back further than the latest failure. Paul takes us back to Adam.

## When Sin Ruled

> Therefore, as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death passed to all men because all sinned.
>
> — Romans 5:12

Through Adam, sin entered the world, and death came through sin. What began with one man reached the whole human race.

As Paul develops this account, sin and death take on the character of rulers. Death reigns. Sin reigns in death. Later, sin will be described as a master whose servants obey it.

This is more than an account of people making separate bad decisions. Those decisions belong to a condition that already holds humanity under its rule. Paul has previously said that Jews and Gentiles alike are under sin. Now he explains how that reign began and how God has answered it.

The law did not introduce sin into an innocent world. Sin was present before Moses, and death already reigned, even over those whose sins differed from Adam’s disobedience. When the law came, it did not bring that reign to an end.

A command can identify what the master demands as wrong. That does not, by itself, release the servant.

God’s answer comes through another man.

Paul places Adam and Christ alongside one another, tracing what comes through each. Through Adam’s trespass came condemnation. Through Jesus Christ comes the gift of righteousness. Through one man’s disobedience, many were made sinners; through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous.

One man’s disobedience made many sinners. The obedience through which many are made righteous belongs to Christ.

> The gift is not as through one who sinned; for the judgment came by one to condemnation, but the free gift followed many trespasses to justification.
>
> — Romans 5:16

God’s gift enters a history already filled with wrongdoing. The many trespasses have not exhausted his grace. Paul says the gift exceeds the trespass.

> For if by the trespass of the one, death reigned through the one; so much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one, Jesus Christ.
>
> — Romans 5:17

Death reigned through Adam. Those receiving God’s abundant grace and gift of righteousness will reign in life through Christ. Both the righteousness and the life come through him.

Paul develops this in verses 18–19: one trespass brought condemnation; Christ’s righteous act brings justification and life. The obedience on which that righteousness rests is Christ’s. The recipient does not become its source.

Then the law enters the comparison. Its arrival brings an increase of trespass, but that increase does not overwhelm God’s provision. Where sin abounded, grace abounded more exceedingly.

> that as sin reigned in death, even so grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.
>
> — Romans 5:21

Grace reigns through righteousness. God has dealt with sin in a way consistent with his own righteousness, as Paul explained when he spoke of Christ’s blood. That provision reaches to eternal life.

Sin and death do not have the final word in this account. Jesus Christ does.

## How Can Those Who Died Continue Living There?

At this point, an objection almost writes itself. If increasing sin has been met by even greater grace, why not continue in sin?

> What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? May it never be! We who died to sin, how could we live in it any longer?
>
> — Romans 6:1–2

Paul’s answer begins with a death that has occurred. Those he addresses died to sin.

Until now, death has appeared as part of humanity’s trouble. Sin entered, death followed, and death reigned. Now Paul speaks of a death that releases people from sin’s bondage. It is their death with Christ.

He explains that those baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death and buried with him. Christ was raised through the glory of the Father so that they too might walk in newness of life (Romans 6:3–4).

Their connection to Christ includes what happened to him. His death and resurrection explain the change in their relationship to the former master.

> knowing this, that our old man was crucified with him, that the body of sin might be done away with, so that we would no longer be in bondage to sin. For he who has died has been freed from sin.
>
> — Romans 6:6–7

The old man belonged to the former slavery. Paul says that old man was crucified with Christ, and he gives the purpose: that we would no longer be in bondage to sin.

This is a decisive break. Paul has not described sin as a master who becomes kinder or makes fewer demands. He describes the person held in bondage as having died with Christ.

The continued presence of temptation does not mean this death failed to occur. Paul will shortly command these same people to refuse sinful desires. He knows that sin still makes demands. His explanation concerns why those demands are no longer to govern them.

That is what the proposal to continue in sin misses. Grace has brought freedom from the very slavery the proposal would continue.

## The Death That Happened Once

The permanence of Christ’s own victory now comes into view.

> But if we died with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him, knowing that Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him! For the death that he died, he died to sin one time; but the life that he lives, he lives to God.
>
> — Romans 6:8–10

Christ dies no more. Death’s dominion over him has ended. He died to sin one time, and he lives to God.

Paul joins the believer’s future to this living Christ. Those who died with him will also live with him. The relationship he describes rests on a death that will not be repeated and a life over which death no longer rules.

This is where the finality of Christ’s work becomes especially clear. His death does not recur whenever someone struggles to understand it. His resurrection does not await confirmation from someone’s experience. What happened to Christ stands accomplished.

Paul therefore writes:

> Thus consider yourselves also to be dead to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus our Lord.
>
> — Romans 6:11

They are to take account of what is true of them in Christ. The instruction follows the explanation of his death and life.

Recognizing that relationship does not create it. Nor does failing to grasp its meaning make Christ’s death incomplete. Paul is bringing their thinking into agreement with what he has described.

For someone occupied with repeated attempts to overcome sin, this changes the starting point. The question has been, “How can I finally release myself?” Paul explains that release through death with Christ, then addresses how those made alive are to live.

## The Old Master’s Demands

> Therefore don’t let sin reign in your mortal body, that you should obey it in its lusts.
>
> — Romans 6:12

Sin still seeks obedience. The mortal body still has desires through which those demands are felt. Paul tells believers to refuse its rule.

He goes on to tell them not to present their members to sin as instruments of unrighteousness. Instead, they are to present themselves to God as alive from the dead, and their members as instruments of righteousness.

Their bodies have a different service to render because they belong to God. The freedom Paul describes reaches into their choices.

> For sin will not have dominion over you, for you are not under law, but under grace.
>
> — Romans 6:14

Paul gives their relationship to grace as the reason sin will not have dominion. God’s provision through Christ has changed the terms under which they live.

Those alive to God in Christ are to give their bodies to his service. Their obedience belongs within the relationship his death and life have established.

In my own struggle, I had repeatedly returned to what I could promise and accomplish. Romans 6 required me to consider Christ’s death and life before measuring another attempt. My struggle could tell me what I had done. It could not establish what God had done through his Son.

God’s action is where Paul begins, and it remains the basis of his instruction.

The question returns in a slightly different form: if believers are under grace rather than law, may they sin?

Paul again answers emphatically that they may not. Presenting oneself to obey a master is service to that master. Sin leads to death; obedience leads to righteousness (Romans 6:15–16).

Freedom from sin does not turn sin into something harmless. Its service still leads toward death. Grace has brought people out of that service, not changed its outcome.

Paul then gives thanks:

> But thanks be to God that, whereas you were bondservants of sin, you became obedient from the heart to that form of teaching to which you were delivered. Being made free from sin, you became bondservants of righteousness.
>
> — Romans 6:17–18

They were servants of sin. They have been made free and have become servants of righteousness. Paul recognizes their obedience from the heart and thanks God for the change.

Their freedom has brought them into his service. As they formerly gave their bodies to uncleanness and increasing wickedness, they are now to give them to righteousness for sanctification. Their lives are to be set apart to God in the service that now belongs to them.

## What Grace Produces

Paul asks his readers to look at what their former service produced. They are now ashamed of those things, and their end is death.

He then describes what has changed:

> But now, being made free from sin and having become servants of God, you have your fruit of sanctification and the result of eternal life.
>
> — Romans 6:22

The two forms of service have different fruit and different ends. Sin leads to death. Belonging to God bears fruit in a life set apart to him and has eternal life as its outcome.

The former service ends in death. Freedom through Christ brings service to God and the fruit Paul has named.

Yet even here, after speaking so directly about obedience, he explains eternal life as a gift:

> For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.
>
> — Romans 6:23

Sin pays what its service earns. Eternal life comes as God’s gift in Christ.

The commands in this chapter have not turned that gift into wages. They address those whom God has released from bondage and brought into his service. Their obedience belongs to the life he has given.

Through Adam, sin entered and death reigned. Through Christ’s obedience comes righteousness, and through union with his death comes release from sin’s bondage. The Christ who died once now lives beyond death’s dominion.

That is why sin’s continued demands cannot be the explanation of the believer’s standing before God. Paul explains that standing through Christ. He then confronts sin on the basis of what Christ has accomplished.

God has not left the former slavery untouched and merely asked for better behavior. He has acted through the death and resurrection of his Son.

But Paul has also said that believers are no longer under law. The law is God’s law. How can release from it belong to his provision—and why does knowing its commands fail to produce the life it requires?

That question takes us into Romans 7.

# Movement 7 — Released to Belong to Christ

*Romans 7*

The law tells us what God requires. It exposes wrongdoing, removes excuses, and leaves the sinner accountable before him. Yet Paul has just told believers that they are no longer under law.

How can that be? God has not withdrawn his commandments or decided that disobedience no longer matters. The law still speaks truthfully about what is right.

Paul explains the change through death.

## A Bond That Death Has Ended

The law has authority over a person while that person lives. Paul illustrates this through marriage. A woman is bound to her husband while he lives. His death ends that bond, leaving her free to marry another.

The obligation was real. So is the release. Death changes the relationship.

Paul then brings the explanation to those who belong to Christ:

> Therefore, my brothers, you also were made dead to the law through the body of Christ, that you would be joined to another, to him who was raised from the dead, that we might produce fruit to God.
>
> — Romans 7:4

Through Christ’s body, they were made dead to the law. They now belong to the one who was raised from the dead.

Their release rests on something that happened to Christ and includes them in its consequences. They did not obtain it by finally becoming successful at keeping the law. They died to it through his death.

And that death brought them into a new relationship. They belong to the risen Christ, with the stated purpose of producing fruit to God.

Release brings a new belonging. Joined to Christ, they are to bear fruit to God.

Paul describes the former condition as life in the flesh, where sinful passions worked through the law in their members and produced fruit for death. Then he writes:

> But now we have been discharged from the law, having died to that in which we were held; so that we serve in newness of the spirit, and not in oldness of the letter.
>
> — Romans 7:6

The release has occurred. Service follows in a new way.

God has dealt with the relationship through Christ’s death. Paul does not describe a temporary arrangement in which the old obligation is suspended until another failure brings it back into force. His explanation is that they died to what held them and now belong to the risen Lord.

They serve as people who belong to Christ.

But why was release necessary? What happens when God’s good command meets a person in whom sin is at work?

## Sin Uses What Is Good

Then the law must be the trouble?

Paul refuses that conclusion. The law made sin known. He names its command against coveting.

Coveting can remain hidden while a person appears outwardly respectable. The command reaches that desire and identifies it as wrong. It tells the truth about something the person might otherwise excuse or leave unexamined.

But sin does more than resist being exposed.

> But sin, finding occasion through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of coveting. For apart from the law, sin is dead.
>
> — Romans 7:8

Sin takes the command as an opportunity. The prohibition against coveting becomes the occasion for coveting of every kind.

The command has not become corrupt. Sin is using what is good to produce what the command forbids. The same sin that Paul has described as a master now appears as an active deceiver.

> I was alive apart from the law once, but when the commandment came, sin revived and I died. The commandment which was for life, this I found to be for death; for sin, finding occasion through the commandment, deceived me, and through it killed me.
>
> — Romans 7:9–11

The encounter with the command did not bring the life Paul expected from it. Sin seized the opportunity, deceived him, and brought death.

Sin produces death through a command that expresses what is right. Its evil reaches even into the encounter with God’s good law.

Paul therefore holds the command and sin apart:

> Therefore the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, righteous, and good.
>
> — Romans 7:12

Nothing in his explanation lowers God’s standard. The law remains good throughout. The trouble is what sin does in the person confronted by it.

Knowing the command has brought the problem into the open. It has not supplied deliverance from it.

## When Wanting Is Not Enough

The conflict comes into his own words:

> For I don’t understand what I am doing. For I don’t practice what I desire to do; but what I hate, that I do.
>
> — Romans 7:15

The person speaking does not need to be convinced that the evil is wrong. He hates it. He wants to do good and agrees that the law is right.

Yet the desired obedience does not follow.

This is why sincerity cannot carry the weight often placed on it. Sincerity is already present in this account. So are knowledge, agreement, and desire. Paul describes a person who approves of the command and still finds himself doing what it condemns.

> For I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, dwells no good thing. For desire is present with me, but I don’t find it doing that which is good.
>
> — Romans 7:18

He locates the failure in his flesh. The ability to accomplish the good is missing even while the desire for it is present.

The flesh here is not simply another name for the physical body. Paul is describing himself in his weakness under sin, where knowing and wanting cannot produce the obedience God’s law requires. The body becomes the place where that conflict is worked out.

Twice he says that the evil he does against his desire comes from sin dwelling in him. He is identifying the power at work within the contradiction. He still calls the actions his own: the evil is what he practices. The explanation brings him to a cry for deliverance.

> For I delight in God’s law after the inward person, but I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity under the law of sin which is in my members.
>
> — Romans 7:22–23

His mind agrees with God’s law. Another power operates in his members and takes him captive.

Paul has already announced release through Christ at the beginning of the chapter. Here he lets the conflict between the good law and sin in the flesh unfold. Its failure explains why that release was needed. The command can be known, approved, and even delighted in while sin continues to overpower the person seeking to carry it out.

Increasing the demand does not answer that condition. The demand is already right. Neither does a stronger declaration of intent answer it. The desire is already there.

The need is deliverance.

## Who Will Deliver?

> What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death?
>
> — Romans 7:24

The question has become personal and urgent. The person who knows the command, agrees with it, and wants to fulfill it cannot release himself from the power he has described.

Paul answers with thanksgiving:

> I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord! So then with the mind, I myself serve God’s law, but with the flesh, sin’s law.
>
> — Romans 7:25

Deliverance comes through Jesus Christ.

The closing sentence still names the division between mind and flesh. Paul has not announced that the flesh has improved or that the conflict disappears through one moment of understanding. His thanks turn toward God through the same Lord to whom believers now belong.

The chapter began with that belonging. Through Christ’s body, they were made dead to the law and joined to the one raised from the dead. The struggle described afterward shows why the good command could not itself provide the life they needed.

God’s answer does not depend on making the flesh a sufficient source of obedience. He has acted through his Son, ending the former relationship through death and establishing belonging to the risen Christ.

The law remains holy. Sin remains sinful. What has changed is the relationship of those joined to Christ, and with it the way they serve God.

Their release does not wait for the struggle to become less painful or for their intentions to become more sincere. Paul grounds it in Christ’s death. The fruit God seeks comes from belonging to the living Christ.

Romans 7 brings the inadequacy of the flesh into full view. Romans 8 will explain the provision of God: what the law could not accomplish through the flesh, God has done through his Son, and the life he gives is lived by his Spirit.

# Movement 8 — God Is for Us

*Romans 8*

The cry at the end of Romans 7 was for deliverance. The law was good, the desire to obey was present, and sin continued to overpower the person who wanted to do right. Paul’s answer was thanksgiving to God through Jesus Christ.

Now he explains what God has done.

> There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus, who don’t walk according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit.
>
> — Romans 8:1

Condemnation has been part of the argument since Paul described humanity’s guilt before God. Through Adam’s trespass came condemnation. Through Christ came the gift of righteousness. Those justified by faith have peace with God, and those joined to Christ have died with him and been raised to new life.

Paul now brings that argument into the life of the Spirit. Those in Christ belong to the one through whom God has dealt with their condemnation. The Spirit’s presence and work belong to that same provision.

## God Condemned Sin

The absence of condemnation does not mean that God has ceased to judge sin. Paul explains it through a judgment God has carried out:

> For what the law couldn’t do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did, sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, he condemned sin in the flesh, that the ordinance of the law might be fulfilled in us who don’t walk according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit.
>
> — Romans 8:3–4

God sent his Son and condemned sin in the flesh.

The law had exposed sin. In the conflict Paul described, sin even used the command to produce death. The flesh supplied no ability to fulfill what the law required. God answered that failure by sending his Son.

God has judged sin. His provision also brings the life in which the law’s righteous requirement is fulfilled.

That life comes through the Spirit. Paul describes the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus as freeing him from the law of sin and death. The power that held him captive in the previous chapter has met God’s answer.

God answers the condemnation and the bondage through his Son and Spirit. The life described here belongs to his saving provision.

## The Flesh Cannot Give Life

Paul describes two ways of living, each with its own direction and outcome. Those who live according to the flesh set their minds on its concerns. Those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on the things of the Spirit.

> For the mind of the flesh is death, but the mind of the Spirit is life and peace;
>
> — Romans 8:6

The connection between sin and death continues. The flesh is hostile toward God. It does not submit to his law and cannot do so. Those in the flesh cannot please him.

God has not improved the flesh until it becomes a dependable source of righteousness. He has given his Spirit.

Paul addresses his readers accordingly:

> But you are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if it is so that the Spirit of God dwells in you. But if any man doesn’t have the Spirit of Christ, he is not his.
>
> — Romans 8:9

Belonging to Christ includes the indwelling Spirit. Paul identifies their relationship through God’s presence in them.

Their bodies are still subject to death. The consequences of sin have not disappeared from creation, and the bodily resurrection is still ahead. Yet the Spirit dwelling in them is the Spirit of the God who raised Jesus from the dead. Paul therefore says that God will also give life to their mortal bodies through his Spirit.

God will give life to their mortal bodies through the Spirit who already dwells in them.

This also explains why the flesh has no claim to their service. It could not provide the righteousness they needed, and it cannot produce the life God gives. Paul tells them they owe it no obedience.

Then he warns:

> For if you live after the flesh, you must die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live.
>
> — Romans 8:13

Paul addresses brothers. The warning belongs to his instruction to those who have received the Spirit, and its seriousness comes from the same connection he has maintained throughout these chapters: sin produces death. The flesh does not become a source of life because someone who belongs to Christ follows its demands.

Death here cannot be reduced to an unpleasant feeling. Paul has described captivity, hostility toward God, and deeds that must be put to death. The choice to serve the flesh gives expression to what God has acted against.

The answer is action by the Spirit. Believers are to put the body’s sinful deeds to death through the Spirit God has given them. The deliverance sought in Romans 7 does not come from discovering a hidden reserve of strength in the flesh.

Paul has already called these readers people in whom the Spirit dwells. He now calls them to live by that provision. The warning explains the deadly character of the service they must refuse; the command identifies the Spirit as the power by whom they refuse it.

He then connects the Spirit’s leading with their place in God’s family.

## Children Who Still Groan

> For you didn’t receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, “Abba! Father!”
>
> — Romans 8:15

God has received them as his children. The Spirit they have received is the Spirit of adoption, and he bears witness with their spirit that they are children of God.

That relationship has an inheritance. Paul calls them heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ, joining suffering with Christ to being glorified with him.

Belonging to the Son includes a path through suffering. Paul makes no promise that adoption removes the hardships of the present world. He sets those hardships beside the glory that will be revealed and says they are not worthy of comparison.

Creation itself waits for that revelation. It remains subject to decay, groaning under a condition from which God will release it. The children of God also groan while waiting for the redemption of their bodies.

They have received the Spirit of adoption, and they await adoption’s bodily completion. Both statements stand together. God has established their belonging while part of his provision remains to be fulfilled.

They are children while they wait. Their bodies remain mortal and their suffering is real, but Paul does not leave their belonging undecided until the waiting ends.

Even their prayers reveal weakness.

> In the same way, the Spirit also helps our weaknesses, for we don’t know how to pray as we ought. But the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings which can’t be uttered.
>
> — Romans 8:26

God’s provision reaches into the inability to express the need adequately. The Spirit intercedes, and the God who searches hearts knows the Spirit’s mind.

The relationship is not sustained by the children’s ability to explain themselves perfectly. God is at work in their weakness while bringing them toward what he has promised.

## God’s Purpose Reaches Its End

Paul now speaks of all things working together for good for those who love God, who are called according to his purpose.

The good has a definite shape. God has purposed that they be conformed to the image of his Son, so that the Son would be the firstborn among many brothers. This is the destination Paul names when he speaks of predestination: God has determined beforehand conformity to his Son.

The suffering and waiting occur within that purpose. They do not supply a different outcome.

> Whom he predestined, those he also called. Whom he called, those he also justified. Whom he justified, those he also glorified.
>
> — Romans 8:30

Those he justified are those he glorified. Paul carries the same people through God’s action to its end.

Glory is still future in the experience Paul has described. Bodies await redemption, and creation awaits release. Yet here he speaks of glorification alongside calling and justification as part of God’s accomplished purpose.

God’s justifying action is therefore not an isolated decision whose outcome Paul leaves uncertain. It belongs to a purpose that reaches conformity to Christ and glory with him.

Romans 5 carried enemies reconciled through Christ’s death forward to salvation through his life. Here Paul carries the justified forward to glory. God’s action joins the beginning to the end.

Paul then asks what can stand against it.

## Who Can Overturn God’s Judgment?

> What then shall we say about these things? If God is for us, who can be against us?
>
> — Romans 8:31

Opposition plainly exists. Paul will soon name persecution, danger, and the sword. His question is what such opposition can accomplish against God’s saving purpose.

God did not spare his own Son. He delivered him up for those Paul is describing. That gift is the basis of Paul’s reasoning: the God who gave his Son will also freely give with him all that his purpose requires.

Then Paul brings the matter before the judge:

> Who could bring a charge against God’s chosen ones? It is God who justifies.
>
> — Romans 8:33

God knew the guilt when he provided redemption. He demonstrated his righteousness in justifying the one who has faith in Jesus.

An accusation cannot undo that work by establishing what God already knew when he provided it. He justified the ungodly through Christ. The charge must reckon with the judgment of the God before whom it is brought.

Paul continues:

> Who is he who condemns? It is Christ who died, yes rather, who was raised from the dead, who is at the right hand of God, who also makes intercession for us.
>
> — Romans 8:34

Christ’s death answers the condemnation. His resurrection brings the living Christ into the answer. He is at God’s right hand and intercedes for those who belong to him.

The one who died is alive before God on their behalf.

There is no unfinished sacrifice in this explanation, no missing righteousness to be supplied by the accused. Paul answers condemnation with Christ’s death, resurrection, and present intercession.

That answer does not become more complete when it is understood clearly or less complete when the person struggles to grasp it. Its substance is what God has done through his Son.

But what happens when someone who belongs to Christ sins again? The wrong must be faced. Paul describes sin bringing shame, drawing people into its service, and harming a brother for whom Christ died (Romans 6:16, 21; 14:15). Each will give account to God (Romans 14:12). Acceptance does not make those consequences imaginary. Neither does a further sin make Christ’s sacrifice an unfinished event. The wrong calls for an honest response and obedience to God; it cannot be answered by offering personal merit in place of the Christ who died and now intercedes.

## Nothing Can Separate

Paul’s final question concerns the love of Christ. Can oppression separate those he loves from him? Can anguish, persecution, hunger, exposure, danger, or death?

These are severe realities. Paul even describes God’s people as sheep being taken to slaughter. His answer does not depend on their escaping what he names.

> No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.
>
> — Romans 8:37

The victory is through the one who loved them, even in the midst of those things. Suffering may take away possessions, safety, and life itself. It cannot accomplish separation from God’s love in Christ.

Paul carries the conclusion beyond the visible threats:

> For I am persuaded that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor any other created thing will be able to separate us from God’s love which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.
>
> — Romans 8:38–39

Death entered the argument as a ruler through Adam. Now it appears among the things unable to separate those in Christ from God’s love. Even death does not possess the authority to defeat what God has established through his Son.

Paul’s conviction follows the reasons he has given. God sent his Son and condemned sin. He gave his Spirit, received children, and appointed their conformity to Christ. He joined justification to glory. He answers accusation as the God who justifies and condemnation through the Christ who died, rose, and intercedes.

The relationship rests on that provision. Its permanence is not produced by the strength of anyone’s conviction about it.

There is still sin to refuse, suffering to endure, and bodily redemption to await. God’s children live through those things within the purpose Paul has described. The future fulfillment is his to accomplish, just as the justification was his to pronounce.

Before God, the matter of condemnation has been answered through Jesus Christ. The one who justifies has not left a second verdict to be earned. The risen Son intercedes, and God’s purpose carries those justified through to glory.

The gospel is God’s power for salvation because through it God saves those who believe in Jesus Christ (Romans 1:16–17). Its power becomes clear as Romans unfolds: God justifies sinners through Christ’s redemption, reconciles enemies through his death, and promises salvation through his life (Romans 3:24–26; 5:9–10). Through union with Christ, those once enslaved to sin are brought into life and service to God. Paul calls them to reckon with what has happened and present themselves to God as alive from the dead (Romans 6:4–13). The gospel therefore remains central to the believer’s life. Faith rests on the God who has acted through Christ, and by the Spirit he has given, believers put sinful deeds to death (Romans 8:13). God’s provision has not changed what sin produces; it delivers people from its dominion and supplies life by his Spirit. God’s saving work reaches from justification to glory. When Paul confronts accusation and condemnation, his answer is God who justifies and Christ who died, rose, and intercedes (Romans 8:30–34). The matter rests on what God has accomplished and promises to fulfill.

**It is settled.**

Yet Paul’s account of God’s faithfulness raises a question that cannot be left outside the discussion. God also gave promises to Israel. What does their present unbelief mean for those promises?

Has God’s word failed?

That is where the letter takes us next.

# Movement 9 — Has God’s Word Failed?

*Romans 9–11*

God justifies, and Christ intercedes. Paul has carried those justified through to glory and declared that nothing in creation can separate them from God’s love in Christ Jesus.

But Israel stands before us with promises from God and a history of unbelief. Paul grieves for his people and prays for their salvation. They received the covenants, the law, and the promises. From them came Christ according to the flesh. Yet many have rejected him.

Has something God established come undone?

If God’s word has failed concerning Israel, what becomes of the future Paul has just announced?

Paul answers directly:

> But it is not as though the word of God has come to nothing. For they are not all Israel that are of Israel.
>
> — Romans 9:6

His answer begins with the promise itself.

## What God Actually Promised

God’s promise never made physical descent sufficient for righteousness before him.

Paul returns to Abraham’s family. The promise came through Isaac, the son God had said Sarah would bear. It continued through God’s call concerning Jacob before the children had done anything good or evil. Its course was established by God’s purpose rather than by human achievement.

The promise was God’s to give and his to fulfill. Its existence did not place him under an obligation to accept every claim made on the strength of ancestry.

Israel pursued righteousness with zeal. Yet zeal could not provide what they lacked.

> For being ignorant of God’s righteousness, and seeking to establish their own righteousness, they didn’t subject themselves to the righteousness of God.
>
> — Romans 10:3

God had provided righteousness through Christ. They were seeking to establish their own.

Their failure to obtain righteousness by works was therefore no failure in God’s provision. It showed the same impossibility Paul had already established for Jew and Gentile alike: works of the law do not justify the sinner.

> For Christ is the fulfillment of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.
>
> — Romans 10:4

The provision stands in Christ. Paul speaks of a message that announces what God has accomplished. No one needs to ascend into heaven to bring Christ down or descend into the abyss to bring him up from the dead. God has sent his Son and raised him.

What remains to be proclaimed is what God has done. The same Lord is Lord of both Jew and Gentile, and everyone who calls on him will be saved.

Israel’s unbelief is serious precisely because God’s provision is real. It rejects the righteousness he gives; it does not expose a defect in that righteousness.

## Unbelief Has Not Ended God’s Purpose

Has God therefore rejected his people?

Paul points first to himself. He is an Israelite who belongs to Christ. He then recalls Elijah, who believed he stood alone amid Israel’s rebellion. God’s answer revealed people he had preserved whom Elijah had not accounted for.

Paul sees the same faithfulness in his own day:

> Even so too at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace.
>
> — Romans 11:5

God has preserved a people by grace. Paul immediately excludes works as the basis of that grace. Its character has not changed because the discussion now concerns Israel.

Nor does the present remnant exhaust his explanation. Israel’s stumbling has become the occasion for salvation reaching Gentiles, and that mercy has a further purpose toward Israel. Paul anticipates their fullness and acceptance. Their present unbelief is not the final outcome he announces.

Paul uses the olive tree to warn Gentiles against boasting over Israel: they stand by faith and will be cut off if they do not continue in God’s goodness (Romans 11:17–22). God is also able to graft the broken branches in again if they do not continue in unbelief (Romans 11:23).

## God Has Not Withdrawn His Word

Paul describes Israel’s hardening as partial and places an “until” within it. He then announces Israel’s salvation through the promised Deliverer:

> “There will come out of Zion the Deliverer,
> and he will turn away ungodliness from Jacob.
> This is my covenant with them,
> when I will take away their sins.”
>
> — Romans 11:26–27

God’s answer deals with the ungodliness. His covenant includes the removal of sins. The promised salvation comes through his provision for the very condition that now causes Paul such grief.

Their present opposition to the Good News has not erased God’s calling. Paul describes them as beloved for the fathers’ sake concerning election, and gives this reason:

> For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.
>
> — Romans 11:29

Unbelief has brought judgment, but God has not withdrawn his calling. The future Paul announces depends on him fulfilling his covenant through the Deliverer.

Israel’s present condition raised a question about the God who justifies. Paul answers it through the promise God made and the purpose he continues to fulfill.

His word has not failed.

He has preserved a remnant, extended mercy to Gentiles, and declared his continuing purpose for Israel. Neither people can claim that God owes them salvation. Both stand in need of mercy, and Paul ends his explanation with God as its giver.

That is why the chapter closes in worship. Who has instructed God? Who has supplied something that obliges him to repay? His saving purpose began with no contribution that made him our debtor.

> For of him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory for ever! Amen.
>
> — Romans 11:36

The God who established righteousness through Christ remains faithful to his word. Israel’s present unbelief has not exposed a weakness in his promises or forced him to abandon his purpose.

What he has undertaken remains his to fulfill.

Paul will now turn to the life that follows from these mercies.

# Epilogue — By the Mercies of God

Paul’s letter continues with instructions about serving, loving, giving, meeting obligations, and receiving people with whom we disagree. After everything he has said about God’s provision through Christ, these commands raise a question.

Is there still something to accomplish before God’s acceptance is complete?

Paul introduces his appeal this way:

> Therefore I urge you, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your spiritual service.
>
> — Romans 12:1

The mercies of God are the basis of the appeal. God has justified freely through the redemption in Christ Jesus. Those justified by faith have peace with him. Paul now calls them to offer their lives in his service.

Their service has a place within what God has established. It supplies no missing part of the redemption.

Paul describes believers as members of one body in Christ, each with gifts according to the grace given. Those gifts are to be used. Teaching, serving, giving, leading, and showing mercy all require willing participation. The grace that provided the gifts gives them their purpose.

Love takes concrete form in that service. It meets needs, welcomes people, honors others, shares their sorrow, and refuses revenge. Responsibility reaches into public life as well: paying what is owed, showing respect, and doing no harm to a neighbor.

These things matter to God. His mercy does not make wrongdoing harmless or obedience unnecessary. But the presence of work in the believer’s life does not turn justification into wages. The life offered to God proceeds from the mercy already received.

Then Paul brings God’s acceptance into an argument over dinner.

> Don’t let him who eats despise him who doesn’t eat. Don’t let him who doesn’t eat judge him who eats, for God has accepted him.
>
> — Romans 14:3

One believer eats; another abstains. Each is tempted to make the other’s practice a reason for contempt or judgment.

Surely the disagreement must be settled before that brother can be welcomed?

God has already received him. Their agreement will not create his acceptance. His acceptance gives them reason to receive one another.

Paul asks who they are to judge another’s servant. The servant answers to his own Lord, and God has power to make him stand. His standing is not theirs to grant or withhold.

The explanation reaches beyond their dispute:

> For if we live, we live to the Lord. Or if we die, we die to the Lord. If therefore we live or die, we are the Lord’s.
>
> — Romans 14:8

Christ died and lived again to be Lord of both the dead and the living. Their belonging rests on his death and risen life. Even here, in a disagreement about food, Christ’s accomplishment governs Paul’s answer.

God’s acceptance does not make our conduct unimportant. The brother Paul tells us to receive is also someone who will give account to God (Romans 14:10–12). That is why contempt, careless use of freedom, and harm to another believer matter. Paul calls accepted people to answer to their Lord for how they live; he does not tell them to earn his acceptance by treating one another well. Their responsibility belongs within the relationship he has already named. The person being treated carelessly over a meal is someone Christ died for.

Paul carries the appeal into the next chapter:

> Therefore accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you, to the glory of God.
>
> — Romans 15:7

Christ has accepted them. Their welcome is to reflect his.

They are to welcome someone Christ has welcomed. Their treatment of him matters because he belongs to the same Lord.

The people named at the close of the letter show this service taking shape. They help, labor, open their homes, and risk their lives for others. Paul honors their work. He also describes his own ministry in terms of what Christ has accomplished through him.

There is no shortage of effort in these chapters. There are needs to meet, responsibilities to fulfill, and dangers to resist. None of that effort becomes the ground on which God justifies the sinner.

That ground has already been established.

God provided redemption through Christ’s blood. He counted righteousness apart from works. He reconciled enemies through his Son’s death and declared that those reconciled would be saved by his life.

When Paul asks who can bring a charge, his answer is that God justifies. When he asks who condemns, he points to Christ who died, rose, and intercedes.

The instructions that follow leave that answer intact.

At the beginning of this book, I recalled being asked what I would say if God asked why he should let me in.

“Because I gave you my life.”

The reply exposed what I had made the basis of my answer:

“I thought He gave His life for you.”

Romans gives my life of service its proper place. I can offer it to God because of his mercies. It cannot accomplish the redemption, provide the righteousness, or complete the sacrifice through which he receives me.

Understanding what God has done does not make it happen. Christ’s death and resurrection do not become complete when their meaning becomes clear to us. The one who intercedes remains Christ, even when the person for whom he intercedes struggles to understand.

There is growth still to take place, obedience still to render, and wrongdoing to acknowledge and turn from. Those matters concern the life of someone who belongs to the Lord. They are not unfinished portions of Christ’s redeeming work.

Our lives have a place in his service. Our righteousness before God rests on his provision.

God has answered the matter of condemnation through his Son. The one who justifies carries those justified forward to glory. Christ lives and intercedes for them.

**It is settled.**
