# It Is Settled

## From God’s Perspective

*A Clear and Simple Tour of the Book of Romans*

> *After the suffering of his soul, he will see the light and be satisfied. ...*
>
> — Isaiah 53:11

**John Wry**
First edition
2026

Scripture quotations are from the World English Bible (WEB), Classic American English edition. Ellipses (...) indicate omitted text.

## Purpose of This Book

What has God settled through Jesus Christ, and what does that mean for the person who believes in him?

That is the question this book follows through Romans. It concerns our sins, our accountability before God, and the standing he gives us through Christ. What has Christ accomplished? On what basis does God justify someone who has sinned? What establishes peace with him, and what gives us reason to say that the matter is settled?

Romans gives these questions substantial attention. Paul explains the human problem before God, what God has done about it through his Son, and what follows for those justified by faith. We will follow his reasoning closely enough to see the grounds for his conclusions.

The purpose of this book is to establish from Scripture that **what God has resolved through Christ does not remain ours to resolve.** The title states the conclusion we will examine. The pages that follow must show why it is warranted.

This is a focused introduction to the argument of Romans, not an exhaustive commentary. Paul addresses many aspects of life before God. We will acknowledge them while keeping our attention on the central question of this book: what establishes our standing before him?

No prior theological training is needed. We will work through the relevant passages in plain language, explaining important terms as they arise and allowing Paul’s argument to develop.

## How to Use This Book

Read with Romans open beside you. When a passage is quoted in part, take time to read the surrounding verses. Notice whom Paul is addressing, the question he is answering, and the reasons he gives.

Follow the argument from one movement to the next. Where a conclusion is unfamiliar—or familiar enough that you have stopped examining it—return to the passage and consider how Paul establishes it. Give difficult statements the same attention as reassuring ones.

The aim is to understand what the text says and why its conclusions follow. By the end, “It is settled” should mean more than a repeated phrase. Its meaning and its grounds should be clear from Scripture.

- # Prelude

  ## Who Defines Reality?

  Before we ask whether it is settled with God, there is another question worth considering:

  **Who gets to say?**

  Who determines what is true about God, about us, and about the relationship between us? Is it what we have been taught? What we have experienced? What seems reasonable? What we feel most strongly?

  We can carry answers to those questions without ever having put them into words. Perhaps we grew up with a particular understanding of God and have rarely examined it. Perhaps an experience changed what we believed about him. Perhaps we accept something when it makes sense to us and hesitate when it does not.

  Even opening a Bible involves a decision about authority. We might gather around a table, read a passage, and discuss what it means without asking why this book should settle anything in the first place.

  That question deserves more than a shrug. If we are going to examine our standing before God through Scripture, we should understand why we are listening to it.

  ## Before We Had an Opinion

  The Bible begins with a statement:

  > In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.
  >
  > — Genesis 1:1

  Within that account, human beings enter a world God has already made. He speaks, creates, names, and calls his creation good. We appear as his creatures, made in his image (Genesis 1:26–27).

  That gives us a starting point with consequences. Our understanding of the world develops within something we did not bring into existence. We can discover, describe, question, and misunderstand it. Our understanding is important, but it does not make us its creators.

  Could the same distinction matter when we think about God?

  If I misunderstand him, have I changed him? If I feel certain that he approves of me, does my certainty establish his approval? If I feel condemned, does that feeling tell me everything there is to know about his judgment?

  Those questions do not make our experiences unimportant. They ask what our experiences can establish. Feeling certain about an answer and having grounds for that answer are different things.

  The distinction becomes especially important when the question is personal: *What does God hold against me?*

  I can describe my conduct. I can remember my failures. I can explain what I intended and how I feel about it now. But how will I know what God says about the matter?

  ## Has God Spoken?

  Scripture presents God as someone who has made himself known.

  > God, having in the past spoken to the fathers through the prophets at many times and in various ways, has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, through whom also he made the worlds.
  >
  > — Hebrews 1:1–2

  The passage identifies both the speaker and the means by which he spoke. God spoke through the prophets and through his Son. That is the claim we encounter when we open Scripture: God has addressed us.

  Paul makes the source and purpose of Scripture explicit when he writes to Timothy:

  > Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness, that each person who belongs to God may be complete, thoroughly equipped for every good work.
  >
  > — 2 Timothy 3:16–17

  This explains why the Bible has authority in the discussion we are about to enter. Its claim is that it comes from God. Its usefulness includes correcting us. We therefore read with the possibility that something we have long assumed may need to change.

  That includes reassuring assumptions as well as frightening ones. An idea does not become true because it comforts us, and it does not become true because it is severe.

  What happens, then, when Scripture challenges something that has always seemed obvious?

  Suppose someone thinks, *God must accept me because I have tried so hard.* Another thinks, *God cannot accept me because I have failed so badly.* These are different conclusions, but each needs examination. What has God actually said about acceptance? What place does he give our works? What has he done about our sins?

  Those are questions Scripture must answer.

  ## Bringing the Question to Romans

  Paul tells Timothy that the holy Scriptures are able to make him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus (2 Timothy 3:15). That brings this discussion directly to the concern of our book. We turn to Scripture to understand salvation and the Christ through whom it comes.

  Romans opens with Paul identifying himself as a servant of Jesus Christ, set apart for God’s Good News. He says that this message was promised through the prophets in the holy Scriptures and concerns God’s Son (Romans 1:1–4).

  The message therefore asks us to consider more than our own assessment of our lives. It directs us to what God promised and what he has done through Jesus Christ.

  This is where the question of reality becomes concrete. When we consider our standing before God, whose account will we trust? Are we willing to examine the assumptions we bring—even the familiar religious ones—in light of what he has made known?

  We do not need to pretend that we have no questions. We need to bring those questions to the text and give its answers careful attention.

  “It is settled” is a substantial claim. It concerns actual sins, real accountability, and our standing before God. Repeating the words will not establish them. We must examine what God has done through Christ and why Paul draws the conclusions he does.

  That is where our reading of Romans begins.

- # Movement 1 — The Good News of God

  *Romans 1:1–17*

  When we open a letter, we may be tempted to move quickly past the greeting. We want to reach the part where the writer gets to the point.

  But Paul’s greeting already tells us something essential. Before explaining our condition or how God deals with it, he identifies the message he has been entrusted to proclaim.

  > Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle, set apart for the Good News of God, which he promised before through his prophets in the holy Scriptures,
  >
  > — Romans 1:1–2

  Paul introduces himself as someone who belongs to Jesus Christ and has been called to carry a message. He calls it the Good News of God. Its promise appears in Scripture before Paul’s ministry begins.

  This answers part of the question we raised in the Prelude. Where will we turn to understand our standing before God? Paul directs us to a message God promised and entrusted him to proclaim.

  Perhaps we have approached that question by considering what seems reasonable. Surely sincerity must count. Surely enough commitment, enough sacrifice, or enough improvement must make a difference.

  But how would we know what is enough? And who would have the authority to decide?

  Paul’s opening gives us somewhere to begin beyond our own estimates. Writing to the Galatians, he explains the source of his message:

  > But I make known to you, brothers, concerning the Good News which was preached by me, that it is not according to man. For I didn’t receive it from man, nor was I taught it, but it came to me through revelation of Jesus Christ.
  >
  > — Galatians 1:11–12

  That is a substantial claim. Paul says that he received the message through revelation of Jesus Christ. We therefore need to examine what he announces, rather than assume that we already know what God would say.

  ## Concerning His Son

  What is this Good News about?

  Paul continues:

  > concerning his Son, who was born of the offspring of David according to the flesh, who was declared to be the Son of God with power according to the Spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord,
  >
  > — Romans 1:3–4

  The message concerns God’s Son. Paul speaks of his descent from David, his resurrection from the dead, and his identity as Jesus Christ our Lord.

  These are concrete claims about a person. As the letter develops, Paul will explain what his death and resurrection mean for those who believe. The answer to our question about standing before God will require us to understand what Christ accomplished.

  It is possible to take Christianity seriously while giving most of our attention to something else: what we have promised, attempted, sacrificed, or failed to do.

  I know something about that.

  I served as a missionary for thirteen years. I was sincere. I wanted to help people, and I wanted to do something for God. I made commitments, promised to do better, and worked at keeping those promises. In many respects, I was disciplined.

  Sometimes I felt proud of reaching a spiritual goal. At other times, I felt guilty and condemned because I had failed to meet my own expectations. I lived through repeated cycles of effort, achievement, pride, failure, and guilt.

  By the time someone asked me a particular question, I was deeply tired—spiritually and ministerially exhausted.

  He asked, “John, if you died today and God asked you, ‘Why should I let you in?’ what would you answer?”

  My answer came quickly.

  “Because I gave you my life.”

  He replied, “How interesting. I thought He gave His life for you.”

  That answer disarmed me. Asked why God should receive me, I had pointed to what I had given him.

  I was not denying that Jesus had given his life. I preached that message. Yet when the question became personal, my answer was about my contribution. My service had become the reason I offered for being accepted.

  The conversation exposed a question I needed to examine: **What was I actually relying on when I thought about my standing before God?**

  My experience could expose the confusion. It could not settle the answer. For that, I needed to understand what Scripture said about Christ and his work.

  Paul’s opening directs us there. The Good News concerns God’s Son.

  ## Why Preach to Believers?

  Paul addressed his readers as beloved of God and called to be saints. He thanked God for their faith and wanted to visit so that they could be encouraged by one another’s faith (Romans 1:7–12).

  Then he wrote:

  > So as much as is in me, I am eager to preach the Good News to you also who are in Rome.
  >
  > — Romans 1:15

  They already believed, and Paul still wanted to preach the Good News to them.

  That is worth pausing over. We can become familiar with the language of Christianity without examining every conclusion we have attached to it. My own answer showed that. I knew the language of Christ’s sacrifice, but I answered a question about acceptance by naming my sacrifice.

  What answer would you have given? What would you have pointed to, and why?

  These questions are not an invitation to measure whether our feelings are strong enough. They help identify the grounds we are relying on so that we can examine them against Paul’s explanation.

  Nor does examining those grounds make service unimportant. Paul himself was a servant. He says that through Jesus Christ he received grace and apostleship for the obedience of faith among the nations (Romans 1:5). His work belongs within what he received through Christ.

  We need to understand that relationship. The fact that someone serves God does not, by itself, explain how that person is justified before him. Romans will address that question directly.

  ## God’s Power for Salvation

  Paul explains why he is eager to preach:

  > For I am not ashamed of the Good News of Christ, because it is the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes, for the Jew first, and also for the Greek.
  >
  > — Romans 1:16

  Paul identifies the power as God’s and its purpose as salvation. He also identifies those to whom it applies: everyone who believes, both Jew and Greek.

  Each part matters. We should not speak of salvation in a way that leaves out faith, or speak of faith as though the power to save originated in the person believing. Paul’s statement holds the two together: the Good News is God’s power for salvation for everyone who believes.

  This gives direction to the question my answer had raised. To understand why God receives a person, we must examine his saving action through Christ. An account of our effort cannot take the place of that explanation.

  Paul continues:

  > For in it is revealed God’s righteousness from faith to faith. As it is written, “But the righteous shall live by faith.”
  >
  > — Romans 1:17

  God’s righteousness is revealed in the Good News. Paul introduces here a subject he will develop throughout the argument that follows.

  How does God deal righteously with people who have sinned? How can he justify them? What does faith have to do with it?

  We need more than a quick definition. We need to follow Paul’s explanation. Otherwise, we may keep familiar words while giving them meanings of our own.

  At this point, we have the opening of the argument: God’s Good News, promised in Scripture, concerning his Son, and powerful for the salvation of everyone who believes.

  My answer had begun, “Because I gave you my life.” Paul’s message requires me to consider what God has done through Jesus Christ.

  The difference is substantial, but we have only begun to examine it. We still need to understand what salvation addresses and why our own efforts cannot supply the answer.

  Paul turns next to the ungodliness and unrighteousness against which God’s wrath is revealed. To understand what has been settled, we must understand the matter that needed settling.

- # Movement 2 — The Truth Exchanged

  *Romans 1:18–32*

  What happens when we know something is true but do not want to live with what it means?

  We might avoid it, find reasons to dismiss it, or surround ourselves with people who agree that it does not matter. The truth itself has not changed. Our response to it has.

  Paul brings us to a far more serious instance of that problem. Having introduced the Good News as God’s power for salvation, he begins to explain why salvation is needed.

  > For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men who suppress the truth in unrighteousness, because that which is known of God is revealed in them, for God revealed it to them.
  >
  > — Romans 1:18–19

  The language is direct. There is truth that God has made known, there are people suppressing it, and there is God’s wrath against their ungodliness and unrighteousness.

  This takes the discussion beyond whether life is working well or whether someone feels satisfied. Paul is describing a problem before God. His judgment belongs to the explanation from the beginning.

  If we are going to understand what Christ has settled, we must understand the seriousness of what stands against us.

  ## What Has Been Made Known?

  Paul explains why he holds these people accountable:

  > For the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made, even his everlasting power and divinity, that they may be without excuse.
  >
  > — Romans 1:20

  The things God made disclose something about their Maker. Paul specifically names his everlasting power and divinity. His conclusion is that those he describes are without excuse.

  He is not saying that creation explains everything the gospel will announce about Jesus Christ. He is identifying what God has made known here and the responsibility that follows from it.

  That distinction matters. The problem Paul describes is not simply that people needed information they had never received. He says that God revealed something of himself, and they did not respond to him accordingly.

  > Because knowing God, they didn’t glorify him as God, and didn’t give thanks, but became vain in their reasoning, and their senseless heart was darkened.
  >
  > — Romans 1:21

  Notice what is missing: honor and gratitude.

  We might expect Paul to begin with the most shocking behavior he can name. Instead, he speaks first of knowing God while refusing to glorify him as God or thank him.

  What does it mean to recognize a Creator while withholding the honor due to him? Can we acknowledge that God exists and still treat him as though he has no claim on us?

  Paul’s words reach beneath a merely verbal acknowledgment. The response he describes concerns how people regard the God they know. Their reasoning becomes futile, and their hearts are darkened. They profess wisdom, yet become fools.

  The problem already involves thought, worship, and conduct together.

  ## Something Takes His Place

  Paul then describes an exchange:

  > and traded the glory of the incorruptible God for the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds, four-footed animals, and creeping things.
  >
  > — Romans 1:23

  They trade the glory of the incorruptible God for images of corruptible creatures. The contrast is between the Creator and what he has made.

  A few verses later, Paul states it again:

  > who exchanged the truth of God for a lie, and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever. Amen.
  >
  > — Romans 1:25

  This helps us understand what it means to displace God. No one has removed him from existence or taken away his authority. People have directed their worship and service elsewhere. Something created receives what belongs to the Creator.

  The exchange is explicit in this passage: images, worship, and service. We should understand that before turning it into a general observation about priorities.

  Still, it raises a question worth considering. When we decide what deserves our trust and allegiance, does God’s claim govern the decision? Or do we give him whatever place remains after our other commitments have been settled?

  The question is personal, but Paul’s point is larger than our sense of balance. He calls this an exchange of truth for a lie. The people involved may continue to worship, but worship itself does not establish that they are honoring God. Its object matters.

  ## God Gives Them Up

  Three times in this passage, Paul says that God gave them up.

  The first follows the exchange of God’s glory for images:

  > Therefore God also gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to uncleanness, that their bodies should be dishonored among themselves;
  >
  > — Romans 1:24

  The word *therefore* connects God’s action to what Paul has just described. Their rejection of God is met by his giving them up to uncleanness and bodily dishonor.

  This is part of a passage that began with God’s wrath. We should not mistake being given over to something for being granted God’s approval of it.

  Paul repeats the statement in verse 26:

  > For this reason, God gave them up to vile passions. ...
  >
  > — Romans 1:26

  He describes women exchanging the natural sexual function for what is against nature and men leaving the natural function of the woman, desiring one another and committing what he calls inappropriate acts (Romans 1:26–27).

  These verses are part of his account of the exchange and its consequences. They are not the whole account. Paul continues into a much wider description of wrongdoing.

  The third statement concerns the mind:

  > Even as they refused to have God in their knowledge, God gave them up to a reprobate mind, to do those things which are not fitting;
  >
  > — Romans 1:28

  Here again, Paul connects their refusal with God’s action. They refuse to retain God in their knowledge, and he gives them up to a corrupt mind and unfitting conduct.

  We do not need to supply a theory about exactly how God does this. Paul’s words are already serious. God is responding to rejection, and being given up does not release people from responsibility for what follows.

  ## A List That Comes Close to Home

  Paul’s description includes sexual immorality, wickedness, greed, envy, murder, strife, deceit, gossip, slander, arrogance, boasting, disobedience to parents, faithlessness, and lack of mercy (Romans 1:29–31).

  It is difficult to keep that list at a comfortable distance.

  We might readily condemn murder while excusing our own envy. We might be disturbed by sexual wrongdoing while treating gossip as harmless conversation. We might object to someone else’s arrogance without examining what we boast about.

  Paul places these things within the same account of unrighteousness. Their inclusion prevents us from reducing the passage to the sins that happen to offend us most.

  Nor does he treat conduct as an unimportant symptom. The acts themselves are wrong. People are harmed, God is dishonored, and those who practice such things are accountable to him.

  Paul closes the description with another reference to knowledge:

  > who, knowing the ordinance of God, that those who practice such things are worthy of death, not only do the same, but also approve of those who practice them.
  >
  > — Romans 1:32

  They know God’s judgment, yet practice these things and approve of others doing them.

  Approval does not change the judgment. Agreement among people does not make their conduct right before God. The passage began with suppressed truth and ends with wrongdoing practiced and endorsed despite knowledge of its seriousness.

  ## The Problem Before God

  What has Paul established?

  God has made himself known. People have withheld honor and gratitude, exchanged his truth for a lie, and worshiped the creature rather than the Creator. God’s wrath is revealed against this unrighteousness, and his response includes giving them up to the corruption they pursue.

  The problem therefore cannot be reduced to an uncomfortable feeling about ourselves. Paul is speaking of actual wrongdoing and God’s judgment upon it. Feeling better would not, by itself, answer that judgment. Neither would finding someone willing to approve what we have done.

  This is why the question of salvation matters. If something is to be settled, it must be settled before the God whose truth has been suppressed and whose judgment has been named.

  But perhaps, while reading, we have been thinking mainly of other people.

  We recognize the exchange. We agree with the diagnosis. We can name examples.

  Does recognizing their guilt establish our innocence?

  Paul turns to that question next. His next words address the person who judges.

- # Movement 3 — No Exemption

  *Romans 2:1–3:20*

  It is possible to agree with everything Paul has said so far and still miss its relevance to ourselves.

  We can recognize dishonesty, cruelty, arrogance, and corruption. We can name people who practice them. We may even have suffered because of their actions.

  But recognizing another person’s guilt does not establish our innocence.

  Paul now turns directly to the person who judges:

  > Therefore you are without excuse, O man, whoever you are who judge. For in that which you judge another, you condemn yourself. For you who judge practice the same things.
  >
  > — Romans 2:1

  His accusation is specific. The person condemning others practices the same things. The judgment he pronounces therefore reaches his own conduct.

  Paul is not saying that every moral distinction is wrong. He is exposing the contradiction of condemning an action in someone else while expecting exemption for ourselves.

  Can we recognize that contradiction in ordinary life? We object when someone deceives us, yet find explanations for our own dishonesty. We resent being treated with contempt, yet excuse the contempt in our own words. The circumstances may differ, but do those differences always justify the different verdicts we give?

  Paul brings the question before God:

  > Do you think this, O man who judges those who practice such things, and do the same, that you will escape the judgment of God?
  >
  > — Romans 2:3

  Being able to identify wrongdoing does not place us beyond judgment. God’s judgment is according to truth (Romans 2:2). It includes what we do, not merely what we condemn.

  ## Patience Is Not Approval

  There is another assumption Paul challenges. If judgment has not fallen, perhaps we take its absence as evidence that all is well.

  Paul gives God’s patience a different meaning:

  > Or do you despise the riches of his goodness, forbearance, and patience, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?
  >
  > — Romans 2:4

  God’s goodness calls for repentance. It gives the person Paul addresses reason to turn from wrongdoing.

  The next verse describes someone who remains hard and unrepentant, accumulating wrath for the day of God’s righteous judgment (Romans 2:5). Continued patience does not mean that the wrongdoing has become acceptable or that accountability has disappeared.

  This matters to the question of what is settled. We cannot establish peace with God merely by observing that life has continued and no immediate judgment has arrived. Paul directs us to ask what God’s patience means and how we are responding to it.

  ## God Shows No Partiality

  Paul then describes God as the one who repays each person according to their works. He speaks of eternal life for those who persevere in doing good and seek glory, honor, and incorruptibility, and of wrath for those who are self-seeking and obey unrighteousness rather than truth (Romans 2:6–8).

  He applies this to both Jew and Greek, then states the reason:

  > For there is no partiality with God.
  >
  > — Romans 2:11

  We must allow these words their full weight. Paul does not make conduct irrelevant. He describes a judgment that distinguishes good from evil and does not favor someone because of their identity.

  That raises a question we cannot skip: if God judges according to works, how will Paul arrive at justification apart from works of the law?

  We need to follow the argument rather than make either statement disappear. Here Paul establishes the impartiality of God’s judgment. By the end of this movement, he will explain the condition of those who stand before that judgment.

  His next distinction concerns possessing the law:

  > For it isn’t the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of the law will be justified
  >
  > — Romans 2:13

  Hearing what God requires is not the same as doing it. Access to the law does not make someone righteous merely because they know its words.

  Nor does being without the law remove accountability. Paul speaks of Gentiles who do things the law requires, showing its work written in their hearts, with conscience and thoughts accusing or excusing them. He brings the discussion to the day when God judges people’s secrets through Jesus Christ (Romans 2:12–16).

  The judgment reaches further than public reputation. Neither possession of the law nor its absence supplies an exemption.

  ## When Knowledge Becomes a Credential

  Paul now addresses the person who bears the name of a Jew, rests on the law, knows God’s will, and is confident of being able to instruct others (Romans 2:17–20).

  These are real privileges. Knowing what God has said matters. Being able to teach it can benefit others.

  But Paul asks whether the teacher’s conduct agrees with the teaching:

  > You therefore who teach another, don’t you teach yourself? You who preach that a man shouldn’t steal, do you steal?
  >
  > — Romans 2:21

  He continues with adultery, sacrilege, and the dishonor brought to God through disobedience. The issue is not whether the person can explain the command. It is whether the person obeys it.

  There is a question here for anyone familiar with Scripture. What do we think our knowledge establishes about us?

  Knowing the right answer can make us useful in a discussion. It does not make an act of disobedience disappear. Teaching someone else to be honest does not answer for our own deceit. Familiarity with God’s words cannot be substituted for doing what they say.

  Paul makes the same point concerning circumcision. He acknowledges its value, then says that breaking the law undermines the claim someone might make on the basis of that outward sign. He concludes by speaking of what is inward, of circumcision of the heart, and of praise that comes from God rather than people (Romans 2:25–29).

  The outward sign does not give its bearer permission to disregard the God to whom it points.

  ## A Real Privilege, but No Immunity

  At this point, we might conclude that Paul has dismissed every distinction as meaningless.

  He anticipates that question:

  > Then what advantage does the Jew have? Or what is the profit of circumcision? Much in every way! Because first of all, they were entrusted with the revelations of God.
  >
  > — Romans 3:1–2

  His answer prevents us from saying that receiving God’s words has no value. It is a privilege to be entrusted with them.

  But privilege and innocence are different matters. Possessing God’s revelation does not remove the need to answer for disobedience.

  Paul also refuses to let human unfaithfulness become an accusation against God. The failure of some to believe does not nullify God’s faithfulness. God remains true (Romans 3:3–4).

  Then he addresses another possible escape: if human unrighteousness makes God’s righteousness evident, would it be unjust for God to judge it?

  Paul rejects that reasoning. God’s righteousness does not turn our wrongdoing into something deserving approval. Nor does good resulting from evil make the evil right (Romans 3:5–8).

  The excuses are being examined one by one. Condemning others does not establish innocence. Knowing the law does not equal keeping it. A religious privilege does not cancel disobedience. God’s faithfulness does not make human unfaithfulness harmless.

  ## All Under Sin

  Paul now gathers the argument:

  > What then? Are we better than they? No, in no way. For we previously warned both Jews and Greeks that they are all under sin.
  >
  > — Romans 3:9

  This is the distinction his argument has been moving toward. There are differences in what people have received, but neither group stands outside the charge of sin.

  Paul supports that charge with Scripture. He describes the absence of righteousness, understanding, and seeking God. He names deceitful speech, bitterness, violence, destruction, and the absence of the fear of God (Romans 3:10–18).

  The accusation concerns people in their thinking, speaking, acting, and relationship to God. It does not require that everyone has committed every sin in the same way or to the same degree. Paul’s stated conclusion is that both Jews and Greeks are under sin.

  We may differ greatly from the person beside us. That difference does not, by itself, answer the charge against us.

  This is where comparison fails as a defense. Finding someone who has acted worse does not explain why our own wrongdoing should be counted as righteousness.

  ## Every Mouth Closed

  Paul concludes:

  > Now we know that whatever things the law says, it speaks to those who are under the law, that every mouth may be closed, and all the world may be brought under the judgment of God.
  >
  > — Romans 3:19

  The law speaks even to those who might have claimed its possession as their defense. They do not stand outside its judgment.

  Every mouth closed is a striking picture. We have heard the judging person, the knowledgeable person, and the person appealing to religious privilege. None of those claims has removed the guilt Paul identifies.

  He then states the conclusion plainly:

  > Because by the works of the law, no flesh will be justified in his sight; for through the law comes the knowledge of sin.
  >
  > — Romans 3:20

  Here is the answer to the question raised earlier. Hearing the law does not justify; doing what it requires matters. Yet Paul has charged both Jews and Greeks with sin, and the law brings that sin to light. Its works do not provide the justification these guilty people need.

  This does not mean that good conduct has no value or that God is indifferent to wrongdoing. The passage has established the opposite. It means that we cannot take the law that exposes our sin and present our record under it as the grounds for being declared righteous before God.

  Perhaps our instinct is still to offer something: better intentions, greater effort, more knowledge, a stronger commitment. Those proposals need to face the actual question. How will they answer for the sin Paul has exposed?

  His conclusion concerns what happens **in God’s sight**. The issue has never been limited to whether we can reassure ourselves or persuade someone else to approve of us.

  We stand accountable before God, and works of the law do not justify us before him.

  That is where this part of the argument leaves us. It is also where Paul turns to explain the righteousness God has revealed through faith in Jesus Christ.

  The next movement begins with his words: **“But now ...”**

- # Movement 4 — God Acts

  *Romans 3:21–31*

  Paul has brought us to a conclusion we cannot improve by comparison: both Jews and Greeks are under sin. The law exposes that sin, and works of the law do not justify us before God.

  What answer remains for someone who stands guilty?

  Paul continues:

  > But now apart from the law, a righteousness of God has been revealed, being testified by the law and the prophets;
  >
  > — Romans 3:21

  The argument turns here. Having explained why works of the law do not justify, Paul announces a righteousness of God revealed apart from the law.

  Yet this does not make the message a contradiction of what God previously said. The law and the prophets testify to it. The Scriptures that expose the problem also bear witness to God’s answer.

  Paul identifies how this righteousness reaches those who need it:

  > even the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ to all and on all those who believe. For there is no distinction, for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God;
  >
  > — Romans 3:22–23

  The absence of distinction continues from the previous movement. All have sinned. Neither Jew nor Gentile can claim exemption from that need. And the righteousness Paul announces is through faith in Jesus Christ, for those who believe.

  He is not describing a second opportunity to establish our own innocence. He is explaining what God provides for sinners.

  ## Justified Freely

  Paul brings three important terms together:

  > being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,
  >
  > — Romans 3:24

  We need to understand what those words contribute.

  First, *justified*. The question at the end of the previous movement was whether anyone would be justified in God’s sight by works of the law. Here Paul describes people being justified freely by God’s grace. The concern remains their standing before God: being counted righteous rather than standing under the charge of sin.

  This is more than a change in how they describe themselves. God is the one who justifies, as Paul will state explicitly in verse 26.

  Then comes *freely by his grace*. The justification is given. Paul has not introduced a new payment the sinner must make or a better record the sinner must first produce.

  Finally, he names its basis: *the redemption that is in Christ Jesus*.

  Grace does not mean that nothing had to be done about sin. The gift comes through Christ’s redemption. Elsewhere, Paul connects that redemption directly with forgiveness:

  > In him we have our redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace
  >
  > — Ephesians 1:7

  The language concerns release from the sins that stand against us. Paul connects it with Christ’s blood and God’s grace. The person receiving forgiveness does not earn it, but the forgiveness is grounded in what Christ has done.

  That distinction matters. Something can be freely given to us without having cost nothing.

  Romans now takes us directly to that cost.

  ## God Deals with Sin

  Paul describes Christ as the one:

  > whom God sent to be an atoning sacrifice through faith in his blood, for a demonstration of his righteousness through the passing over of prior sins, in God’s forbearance;
  >
  > — Romans 3:25

  The blood and the sacrifice keep us from treating justification as a decision to overlook the seriousness of sin.

  What does an atoning sacrifice accomplish? Scripture connects Christ’s sacrifice with dealing with sin itself. Hebrews says:

  > ... But now once at the end of the ages, he has been revealed to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself.
  >
  > — Hebrews 9:26

  Christ offered himself. The sacrifice addresses the sin that made justification necessary.

  This also tells us something about God’s initiative. Romans says that God sent Christ to be this sacrifice. The answer does not begin with sinners finding a way to persuade an unwilling God to receive them. God provides the sacrifice through which he justifies those who believe.

  Paul also explains why this demonstrates God’s righteousness. He refers to prior sins passed over in God’s forbearance. Earlier, we saw that patience must not be mistaken for approval. Here Paul shows that God’s forbearance does not mean he has abandoned righteousness.

  Sin has not become acceptable. God’s answer to it is displayed in Christ.

  Paul continues:

  > to demonstrate his righteousness at this present time, that he might himself be just and the justifier of him who has faith in Jesus.
  >
  > — Romans 3:26

  This is central to the argument of our book.

  God remains just, and he justifies the person who has faith in Jesus. Paul places both together. The justification of the believer does not require God to cease being righteous.

  The accusation was real. The sinner’s inability to establish righteousness through works of the law was real. God’s provision in Christ is also real, and Paul presents it as the demonstration of his righteousness.

  When we say that Christ is sufficient, this is part of what we mean. God himself presents Christ’s sacrifice as the basis on which he righteously justifies the believer. We are not being asked to add enough personal worth to make that action defensible.

  ## What Faith Receives

  Where does faith belong in this?

  Paul has referred to faith repeatedly: faith in Jesus Christ, those who believe, faith in Christ’s blood, and the person who has faith in Jesus. We cannot explain the passage faithfully while leaving faith out.

  But neither should we turn faith into a substitute achievement.

  Imagine someone saying, “I understand that my good works cannot justify me. Perhaps the strength of my believing can.”

  The attention has returned to something about the person: whether their faith is impressive enough to deserve the verdict.

  Paul’s explanation directs us to Jesus Christ and his blood. The saving provision is there. Faith is directed toward the one God has provided; it does not supply another sacrifice or give God a second reason to justify.

  The next chapter will make the distinction between believing and earning even more explicit. For now, Paul tests the matter with a question.

  ## What Is Left to Boast About?

  > Where then is the boasting? It is excluded. By what kind of law? Of works? No, but by a law of faith.
  >
  > — Romans 3:27

  If justification came through our achievement, we could point to that achievement as the reason for our standing. But Paul excludes boasting.

  The sinner does not provide the redemption. The sinner does not become the atoning sacrifice. God provides Christ and justifies the person who believes.

  Paul gathers the conclusion:

  > We maintain therefore that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law.
  >
  > — Romans 3:28

  These words answer the question left open at the end of the previous movement. Works of the law do not justify the sinner. God justifies through faith in Jesus Christ, on the basis of the redemption found in him.

  My missionary answer had been, “Because I gave you my life.” It offered my contribution as the reason God should receive me. Paul’s explanation gives me a different answer to examine: what God has provided through Christ.

  This is not a dismissal of service. It identifies what service cannot supply. It cannot become the grounds of a justification Paul describes as freely given by grace.

  ## One God, One Way of Justifying

  Paul then asks whether God belongs to Jews only. His answer includes Gentiles, because there is one God who justifies the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith (Romans 3:29–30).

  There is no separate arrangement by which one group earns what another receives as a gift. The distinction that could not remove anyone’s guilt does not create two ways of justification.

  Paul closes with another question:

  > Do we then nullify the law through faith? May it never be! No, we establish the law.
  >
  > — Romans 3:31

  Justification by faith does not require us to declare the law false or worthless. Paul has allowed its testimony to stand: it exposes sin, and it bears witness to the righteousness now revealed. The answer in Christ does not depend on denying what the law has said.

  We have reached a substantial point in the argument. God has addressed the sin that stands against us through Christ’s sacrifice. He justifies the believer freely, remains righteous in doing so, and excludes our works as the grounds for that verdict.

  The question is no longer whether we can produce enough righteousness to persuade him. Paul has explained how God justifies someone who cannot present that defense.

  Now he turns to Scripture to show that this is no departure from God’s dealings with those who came before.

  # Movement 4, Continued — What God Counts

  *Romans 4:1–25*

  If justification is by faith apart from works of the law, what should we make of Abraham?

  Paul brings him into the argument directly. If Abraham was justified by works, he would have something to boast about—but not before God (Romans 4:1–2).

  Then Paul asks the question that governs the discussion:

  > For what does the Scripture say? “Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.”
  >
  > — Romans 4:3

  The statement identifies both Abraham’s response and what was counted to him. He believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.

  Paul explains the significance by distinguishing a wage from a gift.

  ## Something Owed or Something Given?

  > Now to him who works, the reward is not counted as grace, but as something owed. But to him who doesn’t work, but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is accounted for righteousness.
  >
  > — Romans 4:4–5

  A wage is owed for work done. Paying it is the fulfillment of an obligation.

  Paul places justification in a different category. He describes someone who does not work but believes in the God who justifies the ungodly. Faith is accounted for righteousness.

  Notice whom God justifies: the ungodly.

  Paul has not quietly replaced the sinner with someone who has first made himself worthy. The one needing justification believes in God, and God accounts righteousness apart from works.

  The distinction is precise. Paul is discussing how a person is justified, not whether a believer will ever work, serve, or obey. We should not expand his statement into the claim that all effort has become unnecessary. The work excluded here is work offered as the basis on which righteousness is owed.

  That distinction reaches the heart of performance as a means of acceptance. If I think God owes me a favorable verdict because of what I have done, I am describing a wage. Paul is describing grace.

  ## Sins Not Charged

  Paul then brings David into the discussion:

  > Even as David also pronounces blessing on the man to whom God counts righteousness apart from works:
  >
  > — Romans 4:6

  He quotes David’s description of that blessing:

  > “Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man whom the Lord will by no means charge with sin.”
  >
  > — Romans 4:7–8

  These words give substance to what it means for the matter to be settled before God.

  There are iniquities to forgive and sins to cover. The blessing does not belong to someone whose record never contained wrongdoing. It belongs to someone whose sins are forgiven and whom the Lord will by no means charge with sin.

  Paul places this beside the counting of righteousness apart from works. The answer to guilt is not a more favorable comparison with another person. It is God’s forgiveness and his accounting of righteousness.

  We should let that distinction remain clear. Forgiveness does not mean the sins were imaginary. Not charging someone with sin does not mean God has mistaken the record. The preceding chapter has explained the redemption and sacrifice through which God acts righteously in justifying.

  The blessing rests on what God does about actual sin.

  ## Before the Sign

  Does this blessing depend on circumcision?

  Paul answers by examining the order in Abraham’s case. Was righteousness accounted to him before or after he was circumcised?

  Before (Romans 4:9–10).

  That order matters. Paul calls circumcision a sign and a seal of the righteousness of the faith Abraham already had while uncircumcised. The sign did not produce the righteousness it sealed (Romans 4:11).

  Abraham therefore stands as the father of those who believe while uncircumcised, as well as of the circumcised who walk in the steps of his faith (Romans 4:11–12).

  Paul is continuing the argument about Jew and Gentile. Neither ancestry nor the outward sign can replace faith. The blessing is not confined to those who bear the sign Abraham later received.

  ## Why the Promise Is Sure

  Paul then turns to the promise. He says it came to Abraham and his offspring through the righteousness of faith, rather than through the law. If inheritance depended on the law, faith would be made void and the promise ineffective; the law produces wrath (Romans 4:13–15).

  Then he states the connection between faith, grace, and certainty:

  > For this cause it is of faith, that it may be according to grace, to the end that the promise may be sure to all the offspring, not to that only which is of the law, but to that also which is of the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all.
  >
  > — Romans 4:16

  Paul does not treat grace as a weakness in the arrangement. He connects it with the promise being sure.

  The promise is not secured by turning it into a reward owed under the law. It is of faith, according to grace, and extends to those who share Abraham’s faith.

  This contributes directly to our argument. We are not waiting until Romans 8 to find reasons for certainty. Here Paul explains why the promise rests on grace and identifies God as the one whose power makes its fulfillment possible.

  ## The God Abraham Believed

  What did Abraham’s faith involve?

  Paul describes God as the one who gives life to the dead and calls things that are not as though they were. Abraham believed the promise that he would become the father of many nations, despite the condition of his own aged body and Sarah’s womb (Romans 4:17–19).

  Paul does not describe faith as empty optimism. It has something specific to rely on: God’s promise and his ability to fulfill it.

  > Yet, looking to the promise of God, he didn’t waver through unbelief, but grew strong through faith, giving glory to God, and being fully assured that what he had promised, he was also able to perform.
  >
  > — Romans 4:20–21

  Abraham’s confidence concerned God’s ability to do what God had promised.

  That is more specific than telling ourselves that everything will work out. Faith has an object and a stated promise. Abraham relied on God to accomplish what the circumstances gave him no reason to expect from his own resources.

  Paul takes Abraham’s believing seriously. He describes its strength and assurance. Yet the ability to fulfill the promise belongs to God.

  The distinction keeps us from treating faith as the power that performs God’s work. Abraham believed the one who could perform it.

  ## Written for Us Also

  Paul does not leave Abraham’s experience in the past.

  > Now it was not written that it was accounted to him for his sake alone, but for our sake also, to whom it will be accounted, who believe in him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead, who was delivered up for our trespasses, and was raised for our justification.
  >
  > — Romans 4:23–25

  Here the argument comes directly to us.

  Paul identifies those to whom righteousness will be accounted: those who believe in the God who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead. He identifies what Jesus’ death concerned—our trespasses—and connects his resurrection with our justification.

  The message is grounded in what God has done. Our sins are named, Christ’s death addresses them, and his resurrection belongs to the explanation of our justification.

  Across these two chapters, Paul has given us much more than a repeated assurance that everything is all right. He has explained justification freely by grace, redemption in Christ, the sacrifice through which God demonstrates his righteousness, and righteousness counted apart from works. He has described sins forgiven and not charged, and a promise made sure according to grace.

  This is the basis on which we must consider our standing before God.

  We do not present our service as an unpaid invoice and ask God to settle it with acceptance. We believe in the God who justifies the ungodly, who raised Jesus from the dead, and who accounts righteousness apart from works.

  Paul has explained the grounds of justification. He now turns to what follows from being justified.

# Movement 5 — Peace with God

*Romans 5:1–11*

What changes when God justifies someone?

Paul has explained that justification is given freely by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus. He has shown from Abraham and David that God counts righteousness apart from works. Now he describes what follows:

> Being therefore justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ; through whom we also have our access by faith into this grace in which we stand. We rejoice in hope of the glory of God.
>
> — Romans 5:1–2

The order is important. Paul speaks of people justified by faith, then says that they have peace with God through Jesus Christ.

They are not trying to achieve enough peace within themselves to become acceptable. Peace with God follows justification.

That brings us directly to the question this book is examining. What has been settled, and what gives us reason to believe that it remains settled?

## Peace with Whom?

We use the word *peace* in several ways. We might mean a quiet afternoon, an agreement after an argument, or relief from anxiety.

Paul specifies the relationship: peace **with God**, through our Lord Jesus Christ.

Later in this passage, he describes our former condition as being enemies and our present condition as being reconciled. That helps explain the peace he announces. Something has changed between us and God through Christ.

The question is therefore larger than whether we feel peaceful.

Suppose someone says, “I know what Romans says, but I still feel as though God is against me.” That feeling deserves an honest hearing. Yet the feeling and Paul’s statement concern different things. One describes the person’s experience; the other describes the relationship established through justification.

We must examine the grounds Paul gives for his statement. He does not say that we have peace with God because we have become satisfied with ourselves. He says that, having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through Jesus Christ.

The reason lies in what God has done through him.

## The Grace in Which We Stand

Paul also says that through Christ we have access by faith into the grace in which we stand.

Grace is not merely something mentioned at the beginning and then left behind. Paul describes it as our present standing. The same Christ through whom we have peace is the one through whom we have access.

Perhaps you have thought of acceptance as a running balance. Obedience adds something; failure subtracts something. At the end of the day, you try to determine whether enough remains for God to receive you.

Paul has already distinguished grace from a wage owed for work. Here he describes justified people standing in grace. He directs them to Christ as the one through whom they have access.

We should not make the word *stand* carry more than Paul says. By itself, an image of standing does not explain everything about the future. Paul gives us a stronger basis than an illustration: he goes on to reason from Christ’s death for us to our salvation through him.

Before doing so, he acknowledges something that might seem to challenge the picture.

These people who have peace with God still suffer.

## Suffering Within the Relationship

> Not only this, but we also rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces perseverance; and perseverance, proven character; and proven character, hope;
>
> — Romans 5:3–4

Paul does not describe peace with God as an escape from every painful circumstance. Suffering remains within the lives of those he has just described as justified.

That matters when we try to interpret what happens to us. The presence of hardship cannot, by itself, establish that peace with God has disappeared. Paul speaks of peace and suffering within the same passage, concerning the same people.

He also describes a progression: suffering produces perseverance, perseverance proven character, and proven character hope. He is explaining why hope can remain in suffering, rather than making the absence of suffering its foundation.

Then he gives the reason this hope does not disappoint:

> and hope doesn’t disappoint us, because God’s love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who was given to us.
>
> — Romans 5:5

God has given the Holy Spirit and poured his love into our hearts. Paul’s explanation includes what God gives within us.

But he does not leave the meaning of God’s love dependent on our ability to describe an inward experience. His next words point to what Christ did and to the condition we were in when he did it.

## When Did God Love Us?

> For while we were yet weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly.
>
> — Romans 5:6

Weak and ungodly. Those are Paul’s descriptions.

God did not wait for the people in this account to establish their worthiness before Christ died for them. Their need was present when he acted.

Paul then considers the unusual willingness of one person to die for another. Someone might perhaps dare to die for a good person. Against that possibility, he places God’s action:

> But God commends his own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.
>
> — Romans 5:8

The demonstration of love occurred while we were sinners.

This answers an important question about its source. God’s love was not first brought into existence by our improvement. Christ died for us in the condition from which we needed rescue.

We can read those words and stop at gratitude for the past. Paul takes them further. What God did while we were sinners becomes the basis of an argument about what lies ahead.

## Much More

> Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we will be saved from God’s wrath through him.
>
> — Romans 5:9

Follow the movement of the sentence.

Christ died for us while we were sinners. We are now justified by his blood. Paul concludes that we will be saved from God’s wrath through him.

He does not move from Christ’s death to uncertainty about whether it will prove sufficient. He moves from what has happened to what will follow.

The words *much more* express the strength of that connection. The God who acted for us in our sinful condition has now justified us through Christ’s blood. Paul gives that present justification as a reason for the future salvation he announces.

This is where the book’s title begins to take substantial shape. The claim is not merely that God has offered a promising beginning. Paul speaks of being justified now and being saved from wrath through Christ.

The future remains connected to the same person through whom justification came.

## From Enemies to Reconciled

Paul states the reasoning again, this time using the language of reconciliation:

> For if while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we will be saved by his life.
>
> — Romans 5:10

Once again, he names the former condition before describing God’s action.

We were enemies. Through the death of God’s Son, we were reconciled to God. Now, being reconciled, we will be saved by his life.

Reconciliation concerns the relationship that was broken. Paul does not describe people persuading God to overlook their hostility by eventually becoming useful to him. Reconciliation came through the death of his Son while they were enemies.

What, then, should we conclude about those who have been reconciled?

Paul’s answer is *much more*. If reconciliation came through Christ’s death while we were enemies, salvation follows through his life now that we are reconciled.

The risen Christ belongs to the answer as fully as the Christ who died. Paul has already spoken of Jesus being raised for our justification. Here he speaks of salvation by his life.

The argument does not end with our past sins being addressed and our future being handed over to our ability to make ourselves acceptable. Paul continues to identify Christ as the one through whom we will be saved.

That is more substantial than saying, “Surely God would not change his mind.” It is the reason Paul actually gives: the death of his Son reconciled enemies, and his life secures the salvation Paul announces for those reconciled.

## Reconciliation Received

Paul closes this part of the argument with a present fact:

> Not only so, but we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation.
>
> — Romans 5:11

We have **now received** reconciliation.

Paul does not leave it as a possibility awaiting enough years of satisfactory performance. He describes something received through Jesus Christ.

There is still a future salvation in this passage. Paul has not said that every part of God’s saving purpose has already occurred. But he gives those justified and reconciled a stated reason for expecting that future: the Christ through whom God has already acted for them.

This is what changed.

Those justified by faith have peace with God. They have access through Christ into grace. They have received reconciliation. Their hope reaches forward because Paul reasons from Christ’s death for sinners to salvation through the living Christ.

The matter is not held open until we can supplement his blood with sufficient worthiness of our own. The blood by which we are justified is his. The death through which we are reconciled is his. The life by which Paul says we will be saved is his.

Our standing before God rests on that provision.

Having established these consequences of justification, Paul now widens the argument. He will consider the sin and death that came through Adam alongside the grace and life that come through Jesus Christ.

- # Movement 6 — His Death, Our Freedom

  *Romans 5:12–6:23*

  Does Paul begin taking back what he has said when he reaches the subject of sin in the believer’s life?

  That question matters. We have read of justification freely given, righteousness counted apart from works, peace with God, and reconciliation received. Paul has reasoned from Christ’s death for sinners to salvation through his life.

  Now he addresses continuing in sin. Does grace turn out to be an arrangement we must complete after all?

  We need to follow his answer carefully. Paul does not begin by placing the believer back where the argument started. He begins with something that has happened: **we died to sin.**

  To understand that answer, we first need to hear the statement that prompts his question.

  ## Grace Reigns

  In the latter part of Romans 5, Paul places Adam and Christ alongside one another. Through Adam, sin entered the world and death followed. Through Jesus Christ comes the gift of righteousness and life.

  Paul emphasizes the difference between the trespass and the gift:

  > The gift is not as through one who sinned; for the judgment came by one to condemnation, but the free gift followed many trespasses to justification.
  >
  > — Romans 5:16

  The gift meets a situation already filled with trespasses. Justification does not arise because the human record has finally improved enough to warrant it. It comes as God’s gift through Christ.

  Paul continues:

  > For if by the trespass of the one, death reigned through the one; so much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one, Jesus Christ.
  >
  > — Romans 5:17

  He identifies those who receive grace and the gift of righteousness, and he directs attention to the one through whom they will reign in life: Jesus Christ.

  The contrast reaches beyond isolated acts. Paul speaks of sin and death reigning, then describes grace reigning through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord (Romans 5:21).

  That is the setting for the question that opens chapter 6.

  ## Has Grace Made Sin Acceptable?

  > What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? May it never be! We who died to sin, how could we live in it any longer?
  >
  > — Romans 6:1–2

  Paul’s question is specific. If grace abounds where sin abounds, should we continue in sin so that grace will increase?

  His answer is emphatic. But notice the reason he gives: *we died to sin.*

  He does not tell believers to produce a death to sin by accumulating enough successful resistance. He speaks of their death to sin as the reason continuing in it is inconsistent with what has happened to them.

  How did this happen? His explanation takes us to Christ.

  > Or don’t you know that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life.
  >
  > — Romans 6:3–4

  Paul speaks of baptism into Christ and into his death, burial with him, and a newness of life corresponding to his resurrection.

  The connection is with Christ himself. The death Paul describes is not an achievement independent of him. It is participation in his death, with new life as its intended outcome.

  This adds something important to what we have already read. Christ’s death is not merely an event we admire from a distance. Paul describes those baptized into Christ as joined to him in it.

  ## What Happened to the Old Bondage?

  Paul continues:

  > knowing this, that our old man was crucified with him, that the body of sin might be done away with, so that we would no longer be in bondage to sin. For he who has died has been freed from sin.
  >
  > — Romans 6:6–7

  The old man was crucified **with him**. Paul gives the purpose: that we should no longer be in bondage to sin.

  He is describing a decisive break with the former slavery. Sin is not presented as an unchanged master under whom God has simply asked us to work harder.

  Yet Paul will shortly address these same people about the desires of their mortal bodies. Freedom from sin’s bondage therefore cannot mean that temptation has ceased to exist or that every decision is now automatically right.

  The distinction is already within the chapter. Something has happened through Christ, and that change gives the basis for Paul’s instructions about how they live.

  We must not turn those instructions into a way of accomplishing the crucifixion he has just described. Our old man was crucified with Christ. The actions Paul calls for proceed from that event.

  ## A Death That Will Not Be Repeated

  Here Paul gives us one of the clearest connections with the subject of this book:

  > But if we died with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him, knowing that Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him! For the death that he died, he died to sin one time; but the life that he lives, he lives to God.
  >
  > — Romans 6:8–10

  Christ dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him. He died to sin one time, and he lives to God.

  These are statements about the finality of his death and the life that follows it. Paul does not describe Christ entering a recurring cycle in which death regains its claim and another death becomes necessary.

  And he has just connected us to this Christ: if we died with him, we believe that we will also live with him.

  That is why the finality of Christ’s death matters here. Paul’s argument about us rests on what is true of him. Our death with Christ is not founded on a sacrifice waiting to be repeated or completed.

  Paul draws the conclusion:

  > Thus consider yourselves also to be dead to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus our Lord.
  >
  > — Romans 6:11

  *Thus* connects this instruction to what he has explained. Christ died to sin once and lives to God; believers are therefore to consider themselves dead to sin and alive to God **in Christ Jesus**.

  Considering this does not make Christ’s death happen. It means taking account of the relationship Paul has described.

  The question is not whether we can make his work real by thinking correctly. It is whether our thinking recognizes what Paul says has happened through him.

  ## The Commands Do Not Reverse the Explanation

  Paul now tells believers not to let sin reign in their mortal bodies. He tells them not to present their members to sin as instruments of unrighteousness, but to present themselves to God as alive from the dead (Romans 6:12–13).

  Notice the description within the command: **as alive from the dead**.

  They are not told to present themselves in order to persuade God to make them alive. Paul addresses them on the basis of the life he has already explained.

  Then he gives this reason:

  > For sin will not have dominion over you, for you are not under law, but under grace.
  >
  > — Romans 6:14

  Grace remains the basis of his argument when he addresses their conduct. He has not exchanged the gift of righteousness for a requirement that they now establish their standing through works of the law.

  This does not make their choices unimportant. The commands themselves show that. But their choices are addressed within a relationship Paul describes as being under grace.

  For years, I struggled with sins that were hidden from people I ministered to. Much of my attention was occupied with the struggle itself: what I had done, how I would prevent it, and whether the next attempt would be different.

  Romans 6 requires me to consider something more than the record of that struggle. Paul directs me to Christ’s death and life, and to what it means to be joined to him. That does not excuse the sin. It changes the basis on which the sin is addressed.

  We will return later to the distinction between rest and obedience. Here the point is the order of Paul’s argument: he explains what has happened through Christ, then calls believers to act in accordance with it.

  ## Made Free, Belonging to God

  Paul raises the question again:

  > What then? Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace? May it never be!
  >
  > — Romans 6:15

  He explains that presenting ourselves to obey someone is service to that master. He names sin leading to death and obedience leading to righteousness (Romans 6:16). Those outcomes give the warning its seriousness.

  Then he thanks God for what has happened among his readers:

  > But thanks be to God that, whereas you were bondservants of sin, you became obedient from the heart to that form of teaching to which you were delivered. Being made free from sin, you became bondservants of righteousness.
  >
  > — Romans 6:17–18

  Paul does not erase their response. They became obedient from the heart. But he gives thanks to God and describes them as having been made free from sin.

  The contrast is between what they were and what they have become. He is not merely offering freedom as a possibility they might eventually achieve.

  He goes on to call for service to righteousness and to contrast the outcomes of the two forms of slavery. Their former conduct brought shame and ended in death. Now, made free from sin and become servants of God, they have fruit of sanctification and the result of eternal life (Romans 6:19–22).

  These statements do not permit us to treat sin as harmless. Neither do they turn eternal life into wages paid for the believer’s service. Paul closes with precisely that distinction:

  > For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.
  >
  > — Romans 6:23

  Even at the end of this discussion of obedience, eternal life remains **the free gift of God in Christ Jesus**.

  ## What Romans 6 Adds

  Romans 6 does not reopen the question of earning what God has given. It explains why continuing in sin contradicts the relationship with Christ that grace has established.

  Paul’s answer rests on a death that has happened, a bondage from which believers have been freed, and a risen Christ over whom death no longer rules. He tells believers to consider themselves alive to God in that Christ and to present themselves accordingly.

  The finality belongs first to Christ: he died to sin one time and dies no more. Paul then brings that death and life into his explanation of our relationship to sin and to God.

  We do not complete his death by resisting temptation. We do not make him rise by improving our conduct. We are called to live on the basis of his death and resurrection, joined to the one whose death will not be repeated.

  That is the contribution of this chapter to *It Is Settled*. The grace that justifies does not leave Christ’s work unfinished. Paul’s appeals to believers proceed from what has happened through him, and his concluding word about eternal life is still **gift**.

- # Movement 7 — Released to Belong to Christ

  *Romans 7*

  If God justifies us apart from works of the law, what relationship do we now have to the law?

  The question follows naturally from what Paul has said. In chapter 3, the law exposed sin but did not justify the sinner. In chapter 6, Paul told believers that they were under grace rather than under law. Now he explains how that change came about.

  His answer begins with death.

  This is important for the argument of our book. Paul does not describe release from the law as something granted when we have finally learned enough, struggled enough, or become sufficiently obedient. He connects it to what happened through Christ.

  ## A Relationship Changed by Death

  Paul begins with a principle familiar to those who know the law:

  > Or don’t you know, brothers (for I speak to men who know the law), that the law has dominion over a man for as long as he lives?
  >
  > — Romans 7:1

  He illustrates the point through marriage. A married woman is bound by law to her husband while he lives. If he dies, she is released from that binding relationship and may be joined to another without being an adulteress (Romans 7:2–3).

  The illustration concerns a genuine obligation and a death that changes it. The woman is not released because she decides the obligation no longer suits her. Death has changed her relationship to it.

  Paul then applies the principle:

  > Therefore, my brothers, you also were made dead to the law through the body of Christ, that you would be joined to another, to him who was raised from the dead, that we might produce fruit to God.
  >
  > — Romans 7:4

  We should follow the application Paul actually makes. He does not say that the law died. He says that **we were made dead to the law through the body of Christ**.

  The means of release is Christ’s death. Its purpose includes a new relationship: being joined to the one raised from the dead.

  Here again, Paul places Christ’s death and resurrection at the center of what has changed for believers. The explanation does not begin with their achievement. It begins with their participation in what happened through him.

  Nor does release leave them without belonging. They are joined to Christ.

  ## Discharged from the Law

  Paul contrasts the former situation with the present one. When they were in the flesh, sinful passions operating through the law worked in their members to produce fruit for death (Romans 7:5).

  Then comes the contrast:

  > But now we have been discharged from the law, having died to that in which we were held; so that we serve in newness of the spirit, and not in oldness of the letter.
  >
  > — Romans 7:6

  *We have been discharged.* Paul describes a release that has occurred, and he gives its basis: having died to that in which we were held.

  That is a substantial statement about what has been settled. Their former relationship to the law has changed through death. Paul does not describe it as an obligation temporarily suspended while God waits to see whether they will perform well enough.

  The release also has a purpose: service in newness of the spirit.

  This order matters. Service is not presented as the payment that obtains release. Paul describes release through death and then the new manner of service that follows.

  We can therefore take both statements seriously. Believers have been released, and believers serve. The service does not erase the release or turn it into an unfinished task.

  For this book, the central point is the basis of the change. Paul directs us to the body of Christ and to belonging to the risen Lord. He does not direct us back to our record under the law as the means of establishing our standing.

  ## Is Something Wrong with the Law?

  Paul’s explanation raises an obvious question. If sinful passions worked through the law and release from it was necessary, does that make the law sinful?

  He answers immediately:

  > What shall we say then? Is the law sin? May it never be! However, I wouldn’t have known sin except through the law. For I wouldn’t have known coveting unless the law had said, “You shall not covet.”
  >
  > — Romans 7:7

  The law identifies sin. Paul chooses coveting, a desire that may remain hidden from other people while still being known to the person who wants what is forbidden.

  The command names the wrong. But another actor appears in Paul’s explanation:

  > But sin, finding occasion through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of coveting. For apart from the law, sin is dead.
  >
  > — Romans 7:8

  Sin takes an opportunity through the commandment.

  Paul distinguishes the command from what sin does with it. The command forbids coveting; sin produces coveting. The command is good; sin uses it in bringing about death.

  He continues:

  > I was alive apart from the law once, but when the commandment came, sin revived and I died. The commandment which was for life, this I found to be for death; for sin, finding occasion through the commandment, deceived me, and through it killed me.
  >
  > — Romans 7:9–11

  The explanation is more serious than a cycle of feeling guilty and trying harder. Paul speaks of deception and death. Sin uses the very command that identifies what is right.

  He nevertheless refuses to blame the law:

  > Therefore the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, righteous, and good.
  >
  > — Romans 7:12

  Release from the law does not require us to call it defective or evil. Its goodness remains. The problem is sin, which Paul says produces death through what is good, revealing its exceedingly sinful character (Romans 7:13).

  That distinction protects the argument from a mistaken conclusion. God has not solved the human problem by deciding that righteousness no longer matters. The command remains righteous. What we need cannot be supplied merely by hearing that righteous command again.

  ## Wanting the Good

  Paul now describes a conflict that many readers recognize:

  > For I don’t understand what I am doing. For I don’t practice what I desire to do; but what I hate, that I do.
  >
  > — Romans 7:15

  There is no satisfaction with the evil here. The person speaking desires the good and hates what he does.

  That makes the passage especially searching. It describes more than ignorance of right and wrong. Agreement with the good is present, yet the desired action does not follow.

  Can we recognize the difference? We may sincerely condemn what we have done. We may wish we had acted otherwise. We may even explain clearly what the right action would have been. None of those things is identical to having done it.

  Paul puts the distinction plainly:

  > For I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, dwells no good thing. For desire is present with me, but I don’t find it doing that which is good.
  >
  > — Romans 7:18

  The qualification matters: *in me, that is, in my flesh*. Paul identifies the place in which he finds no good thing and distinguishes desire from accomplishment.

  The passage will not allow us to make sincerity the sufficient answer. The desire is already there.

  ## Whose Experience Is This?

  We should pause over how Paul describes himself in this part of the chapter.

  He speaks in the present tense. He says that he delights in God’s law inwardly, yet also describes himself as fleshly, sold under sin, and brought into captivity to sin’s law (Romans 7:14, 22–23).

  Those descriptions require careful reading alongside chapter 6, where he speaks of believers as made free from sin, and the opening of chapter 7, where he says they have been discharged from the law.

  The language of delight and present conflict can be read as a believer’s experience of continuing struggle. The language of slavery and captivity can be read as Paul portraying the condition from which deliverance is needed. This paragraph does not explicitly date the experience for us.

  We need not settle that question by forcing every expression into a description of all believers at all times. The point needed here is clear in either case: knowing, approving, and desiring the good do not themselves overcome sin’s power. The law’s goodness does not turn the flesh into an adequate source of obedience.

  That is the distinction Paul has been developing. The problem is not repaired by discovering that the command was wrong. Neither is it repaired simply by agreeing that the command was right.

  ## Sin Within, and the Need for Deliverance

  Paul twice says that when he does what he does not desire, it is sin dwelling in him that does it (Romans 7:17, 20).

  We should not turn that explanation into an excuse: “It was not really me, so I bear no responsibility.” Paul continues to describe the deeds as what he practices, and the condition brings him to a cry for deliverance.

  He is exposing sin’s activity within the conflict. The presence of a desire for good does not mean that sin has ceased to act.

  His description reaches its sharpest point here:

  > For I delight in God’s law after the inward person, but I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity under the law of sin which is in my members.
  >
  > — Romans 7:22–23

  Delight in the law and captivity to sin stand together in the description. This is why more instruction, by itself, cannot be the whole answer. The person speaking already recognizes the law as good.

  The question becomes:

  > What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death?
  >
  > — Romans 7:24

  He asks for a deliverer.

  That does not make knowledge, desire, or responsibility meaningless. It shows their insufficiency to accomplish the deliverance being sought. The passage has exposed a need that approval of the command cannot meet.

  Paul answers:

  > I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord! So then with the mind, I myself serve God’s law, but with the flesh, sin’s law.
  >
  > — Romans 7:25

  Thanksgiving is directed to God through Jesus Christ. Paul then summarizes the division he has described, so we should not turn this verse into a claim that every experience of conflict has suddenly disappeared.

  His explanation continues into the next chapter, where he will describe what God did that the law could not do because of the flesh.

  ## What This Establishes

  Romans 7 contributes something essential to *It Is Settled*: the believer’s release from the law is grounded in Christ’s death, not in the believer’s eventual success under it.

  Paul states that release before describing the law’s goodness and the conflict involving sin. We should not lose the opening in the intensity of the later paragraphs.

  Believers were made dead to the law through the body of Christ. They were joined to the risen Lord. They have been discharged from that in which they were held, with service in newness of the spirit as the stated purpose.

  The chapter therefore does not send us back to the law to obtain the standing Christ has provided. Nor does it tell us to solve the conflict by pretending that the command is unimportant. It preserves the command’s goodness while exposing what sin does and what the flesh cannot accomplish.

  This is more than a change of motivation. Paul’s answer is not simply to exchange unpleasant feelings for encouraging ones. A death has changed the relationship, a risen Lord is the one to whom believers belong, and deliverance comes through Jesus Christ.

  Our struggle must be understood in light of that explanation. It cannot be made the means by which we complete the death that released us or earn the belonging Paul has already described.

  The next movement develops the answer further: what the law could not do, God did through his Son.

- # Movement 8 — God Is for Us

  *Romans 8*

  By the time we reach Romans 8, Paul has already given substantial reasons for the conclusion we are examining.

  God justifies freely through the redemption in Christ Jesus. He counts righteousness apart from works. Those justified by faith have peace with him. Christ’s death reconciled them while they were enemies, and Paul has reasoned from that reconciliation to salvation by his life.

  Believers have also been joined to Christ in his death and resurrection. They have been made dead to the law through his body and joined to the risen Lord.

  Romans 8 gathers these truths and carries the argument forward. Paul considers life in the Spirit, our present suffering, and the future God has purposed. Then he asks the questions that bring our standing before God into sharp focus.

  Who can bring a charge? Who condemns? What can separate us from God’s love?

  His answers rest on what God has done through Christ.

  ## No Condemnation

  > There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus, who don’t walk according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit.
  >
  > — Romans 8:1

  Paul identifies those of whom he speaks: people in Christ Jesus, walking according to the Spirit rather than the flesh. We should preserve that description as carefully as we preserve the words *no condemnation*.

  The statement is not an announcement that God has stopped distinguishing good from evil. Paul explains what God has done about sin:

  > For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus made me free from the law of sin and of death. For what the law couldn’t do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did, sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, he condemned sin in the flesh,
  >
  > — Romans 8:2–3

  What the law could not do, God did.

  That answers the need exposed in the previous chapter. The law was good, but the flesh did not supply what was needed. God acted by sending his Son, and Paul says that God condemned sin in the flesh.

  The absence of condemnation for those in Christ is therefore connected to God’s action against sin. It does not depend on pretending that sin never mattered.

  Paul continues by describing the fulfillment of the law’s ordinance in those who walk according to the Spirit (Romans 8:4). God’s provision establishes a relationship to him that includes a different manner of life.

  The explanation begins with God’s action through his Son.

  ## Belonging to Christ

  Paul contrasts the mind of the flesh with the mind of the Spirit. The former is hostile to God; the latter is life and peace. Those in the flesh cannot please God (Romans 8:5–8).

  Then he addresses his readers:

  > But you are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if it is so that the Spirit of God dwells in you. But if any man doesn’t have the Spirit of Christ, he is not his.
  >
  > — Romans 8:9

  Belonging to Christ and having his Spirit are connected. Paul is not describing a change achieved merely by adopting a more hopeful outlook.

  The Spirit dwelling in believers also connects their present life with resurrection:

  > But if the Spirit of him who raised up Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised up Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you.
  >
  > — Romans 8:11

  Their bodies are still mortal. Death has not disappeared from their present experience. Yet Paul points to what God will do, grounding that future in the Spirit who already dwells in them and in God’s raising of Jesus.

  Once again, the argument moves from what God has done to what he will do.

  “It is settled” does not mean that everything promised has already been experienced. It means we must examine how God’s completed action establishes the future Paul announces.

  ## The Warning and the Gift

  Paul’s assurance does not make his next words unnecessary:

  > For if you live after the flesh, you must die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live.
  >
  > — Romans 8:13

  These are serious alternatives. Paul addresses his readers as brothers and calls them away from living according to the flesh. We cannot turn the warning into a harmless observation or detach life in the Spirit from belonging to Christ.

  Neither does he tell them to earn life by an independent supply of moral strength. The action he describes is **by the Spirit**. The God who acted through his Son has also given his Spirit.

  Paul’s assurance concerns people in Christ, indwelt and led by the Spirit. It is not a promise that a life governed by the flesh leads to the same end. The warning belongs within his explanation of the life God gives.

  Then Paul describes the relationship in which believers stand:

  > For you didn’t receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, “Abba! Father!” The Spirit himself testifies with our spirit that we are children of God;
  >
  > — Romans 8:15–16

  They have received the Spirit of adoption. They address God as Father. The Spirit testifies that they are God’s children.

  Paul has moved beyond describing acquittal from a charge. He speaks of belonging within God’s family.

  This is something received, and it gives content to the relationship established through Christ. Those whom God has justified are not described here as strangers hoping eventually to gain admission.

  ## Children, Heirs, and Sufferers

  Paul continues:

  > and if children, then heirs—heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ, if indeed we suffer with him, that we may also be glorified with him.
  >
  > — Romans 8:17

  The promise includes suffering with Christ and being glorified with him. Paul does not offer an inheritance detached from that relationship, nor does he suggest that being God’s children removes every painful experience.

  Instead, he compares present sufferings with the glory to be revealed. Creation itself groans, waiting for deliverance from decay. Believers also groan while awaiting the redemption of their bodies (Romans 8:18–23).

  This gives us a necessary distinction. We have received the Spirit of adoption, yet we await the bodily completion Paul also calls adoption. We belong to God while still waiting for something he has promised.

  Waiting does not mean that nothing has been established.

  A person may ask, “If God is for me, why is life still so painful?” Paul does not answer by denying the pain. He places suffering within the account of those who are God’s children and heirs.

  The promised glory is not inferred from pleasant circumstances. Its grounds lie elsewhere.

  ## Help in Weakness

  Paul also acknowledges weakness in prayer:

  > In the same way, the Spirit also helps our weaknesses, for we don’t know how to pray as we ought. But the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings which can’t be uttered.
  >
  > — Romans 8:26

  Even here, Paul does not describe believers as left to sustain the relationship through their own adequacy. The Spirit helps in weakness and intercedes for the saints according to God (Romans 8:27).

  This is not a guarantee that prayer will always feel easy or that we will always know what to ask. Paul expressly acknowledges that we do not.

  His answer is the Spirit’s help.

  The chapter’s assurance makes room for suffering, waiting, groaning, and weakness. None of those experiences is, by itself, evidence that God’s purpose has failed.

  ## From God’s Purpose to Glory

  Paul now identifies the people for whom all things work together for good: those who love God, who are called according to his purpose (Romans 8:28).

  He then explains that purpose:

  > For whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brothers.
  >
  > — Romans 8:29

  The good Paul describes must be read in light of this purpose. God intends conformity to his Son. Paul has not promised that every event is pleasant or that every desire will be fulfilled.

  Then comes the sequence:

  > Whom he predestined, those he also called. Whom he called, those he also justified. Whom he justified, those he also glorified.
  >
  > — Romans 8:30

  Follow the repeated connection: *whom … those he also*.

  Paul does not introduce one group at justification and quietly substitute another at glorification. He carries the people he describes through the sequence of God’s action. Those he justified, he also glorified.

  Earlier in the chapter, glory was still to be revealed and bodily redemption was still awaited. Paul has not forgotten that future. Here he speaks of glorification as part of the same divine action that includes calling and justification.

  This gives substantial force to the certainty of his argument. Justification is not presented as an isolated beginning with no connection to God’s intended end. Paul connects the justified with the glorified.

  We do not need to add a theory to appreciate what the sentence says. God’s purpose reaches beyond the present, and Paul explicitly joins justification to its glorious outcome.

  ## Who Can Bring a Charge?

  Paul now asks what follows from all this:

  > What then shall we say about these things? If God is for us, who can be against us?
  >
  > — Romans 8:31

  He is not claiming that believers have no opponents. The chapter will shortly name persecution, danger, and death. His question concerns what those opponents can accomplish against the people for whom God acts.

  He gives a reason:

  > He who didn’t spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how would he not also with him freely give us all things?
  >
  > — Romans 8:32

  The gift of the Son is the grounds of the argument. As in chapter 5, Paul reasons from God’s costly action already accomplished to the provision that follows.

  Then he brings us directly to accusation:

  > Who could bring a charge against God’s chosen ones? It is God who justifies.
  >
  > — Romans 8:33

  The answer is not that no accusation could ever describe a real failure. Paul has already established human guilt and explained justification for the ungodly.

  His answer is the identity and action of the judge: **God justifies.**

  An accusation cannot be treated as the final verdict while ignoring God’s justifying action. The question of standing before him is answered by what he has done through Christ.

  Paul continues:

  > Who is he who condemns? It is Christ who died, yes rather, who was raised from the dead, who is at the right hand of God, who also makes intercession for us.
  >
  > — Romans 8:34

  Each statement contributes to the answer. Christ died. He was raised. He is at God’s right hand. He intercedes for us.

  Paul does not direct the accused person to produce a better record than the accuser expects. He directs attention to the Christ whose death and resurrection have already been explained as the grounds of justification.

  The living Christ is also the interceding Christ. God’s provision is not described as a past event followed by present indifference.

  This is why “no condemnation” is more than a reassuring phrase. Paul gives its grounds.

  ## What Can Separate Us?

  His final question concerns separation:

  > Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Could oppression, or anguish, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?
  >
  > — Romans 8:35

  These are circumstances in which someone might be tempted to conclude that God’s love has failed. Paul does not deny that believers encounter them. He includes a scriptural description of God’s people being killed and regarded as sheep for slaughter.

  Then he answers:

  > No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.
  >
  > — Romans 8:37

  *In all these things.* The answer does not depend on escaping every suffering he named.

  Paul’s conclusion reaches further still:

  > For I am persuaded that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor any other created thing will be able to separate us from God’s love which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.
  >
  > — Romans 8:38–39

  Present suffering and future threats do not possess the power to separate those of whom Paul speaks from God’s love in Christ.

  His confidence has reasons. God gave his Son. God justifies. Christ died, rose, and intercedes. God’s purpose joins justification to glory.

  The conclusion gathers the argument rather than replacing it.

  ## The Answer Before God

  We began by asking what establishes our standing before God and why we can say that the matter is settled.

  Paul has now brought the question to accusation, condemnation, and separation. In each case, his answer directs us to God’s action through Christ.

  The sin was real. God did not pretend otherwise. He provided redemption through Christ’s blood and justified those who believe. He reconciled enemies through his Son’s death. He joined believers to Christ, gave them his Spirit, and described them as his children and heirs.

  Now Paul connects those justified with glory and answers the condemning charge with Christ’s death, resurrection, and intercession.

  That is the substance beneath the title of this book.

  There is still suffering. There is still bodily redemption to await. There are commands to heed and sin to put to death by the Spirit. But Paul does not leave our standing to be established by completing what Christ failed to accomplish.

  God is the one who justifies. Christ is the one who died and rose, and who intercedes for us. No created thing can overturn the love of God in Christ that Paul has described.

  **It is settled because of what God has done through Jesus Christ.**

# Final Chapter — Has God’s Word Failed?

*Romans 9–11*

If our standing rests on what God has promised and accomplished, his faithfulness matters at every point.

That makes Israel’s place in Paul’s argument important. God gave Israel covenants and promises. Yet Paul begins these chapters with sorrow for his own people and later expresses his desire that they may be saved.

How do those things belong together? Has God failed to accomplish what he said? Has human unbelief defeated his purpose?

Paul addresses the question directly:

> But it is not as though the word of God has come to nothing. For they are not all Israel that are of Israel.
>
> — Romans 9:6

His answer is not a general appeal to an optimistic reading of history. He examines the promises, distinguishes physical descent from the children of promise, addresses unbelief, and explains why Israel’s present condition does not mean God has abandoned his people.

We need to follow those distinctions. Otherwise, we might make Israel’s history prove something Paul himself does not claim.

## What Did God Promise?

Paul first identifies Israel’s privileges: adoption, glory, covenants, the giving of the law, service, and promises. From Israel came the fathers and, according to the flesh, Christ himself (Romans 9:4–5).

Those privileges are substantial. Paul’s sorrow does not make them imaginary.

But he does not conclude that every physical descendant of Abraham therefore possesses everything promised. He recalls the distinction concerning Isaac and explains:

> That is, it is not the children of the flesh who are children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as heirs.
>
> — Romans 9:8

The distinction belongs within the scriptural account itself. God’s promise cannot be evaluated as though it said something different from what God actually declared.

That is an important principle for our own reading. Before deciding that God has failed to keep a promise, we must establish what he promised and to whom.

Paul then considers Jacob and Esau. He locates God’s purpose before their birth and before either had done good or evil, describing it as depending on the one who calls rather than on works (Romans 9:10–12).

His argument directs attention to God’s purpose and mercy. The promise is not a reward that human achievement first obliges God to provide.

## Mercy Is Not a Debt

Paul anticipates an objection: does this make God unrighteous?

His answer is emphatic. He recalls God’s declaration to Moses that he will have mercy and compassion on whom he will, then concludes:

> So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy.
>
> — Romans 9:16

Mercy is God’s to give. Paul does not describe human desire or exertion as placing God under obligation.

He also speaks of hardening and uses the image of a potter’s authority over clay. These are serious claims about God’s right to act. Paul’s account includes patience, wrath, judgment, and mercy; we should not reduce it to a comforting statement that everyone’s outcome is the same.

He identifies those called as coming from both Jews and Gentiles, then brings forward scriptural testimony concerning people being called God’s people and a remnant of Israel being saved (Romans 9:24–29).

His answer to the question about God’s word is therefore taking shape. God’s purpose includes distinctions already expressed in Scripture. The calling of Gentiles and the preservation of a remnant are not evidence that his word has collapsed.

## Seeking to Establish Their Own Righteousness

Paul also addresses human response. His discussion of God’s purpose does not make unbelief disappear.

At the end of chapter 9, he contrasts Gentiles who attained righteousness by faith with Israel’s pursuit of a law of righteousness. Why did Israel not arrive?

Because they pursued it as though it were by works rather than by faith (Romans 9:30–32).

Paul develops the point:

> For being ignorant of God’s righteousness, and seeking to establish their own righteousness, they didn’t subject themselves to the righteousness of God.
>
> — Romans 10:3

This connects directly with the argument we have followed throughout the book.

Zeal for God is not itself the righteousness God provides. Paul acknowledges their zeal, yet says it is not according to knowledge. Their pursuit of their own righteousness leaves them resisting the righteousness of God.

He then directs attention to Christ:

> For Christ is the fulfillment of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.
>
> — Romans 10:4

The answer has not changed since Romans 3. Righteousness is found in relation to Christ and faith, rather than established by presenting our own achievement.

Paul goes on to speak of confessing Jesus as Lord and believing that God raised him from the dead. He again removes the distinction between Jew and Greek: the same Lord is Lord of all and is rich toward all who call on him (Romans 10:9–13).

The message is proclaimed so that people may hear and believe. Paul also acknowledges that not all have obeyed the Good News.

God’s faithfulness does not require us to call unbelief faith. The promise and the response must both be read as Paul describes them.

## Has God Rejected His People?

At the opening of chapter 11, Paul asks:

> I ask then, did God reject his people? May it never be! For I also am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin.
>
> — Romans 11:1

Paul himself is part of the answer. An Israelite who belongs to Christ cannot be evidence that God has rejected every Israelite.

He then recalls Elijah, who thought he was alone. God’s answer identified people he had preserved. Paul applies that account to his own time:

> Even so too at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace.
>
> — Romans 11:5

The remnant demonstrates that rejection is not total. And its existence is according to grace.

Paul makes the distinction explicit:

> And if by grace, then it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace. But if it is of works, it is no longer grace; otherwise work is no longer work.
>
> — Romans 11:6

The principle governing justification remains visible in the account of Israel. Grace cannot be quietly converted into wages without changing its character.

Yet Paul also says that the rest were hardened. His explanation includes both the preserved remnant and the reality of judgment.

God’s purpose has not failed, but that does not mean every person’s response is inconsequential.

## The Warning Against Boasting

Paul next addresses Gentiles. Israel’s stumbling has been the occasion for salvation reaching them, but this gives them no grounds for superiority.

He uses the image of an olive tree. Some natural branches have been broken off, and a wild olive branch has been grafted in to share the nourishment of the root.

The grafted branch does not support the root. The root supports it (Romans 11:17–18).

Paul explains the distinction:

> True; by their unbelief they were broken off, and you stand by your faith. Don’t be conceited, but fear;
>
> — Romans 11:20

Then he states the warning:

> See then the goodness and severity of God. Toward those who fell, severity; but toward you, goodness, if you continue in his goodness; otherwise you also will be cut off.
>
> — Romans 11:22

We cannot use these chapters as proof that unbelief merely changes someone’s feelings while leaving every relationship untouched. Paul describes branches broken off because of unbelief and warns those addressed not to presume upon their place.

The immediate argument concerns Israel and Gentile participation in the blessing. It does not explicitly identify every branch with an individually justified person, nor does it explain every implication of being cut off. We should not settle those questions by assumption.

What it does make clear is that Gentile inclusion gives no warrant for boasting and that standing is by faith. God’s faithfulness must not be turned into a reason to disregard his warning.

Paul also refuses to treat the broken-off branches as beyond hope:

> They also, if they don’t continue in their unbelief, will be grafted in, for God is able to graft them in again.
>
> — Romans 11:23

Unbelief is serious, and God is able to graft them in again. Both belong to Paul’s explanation.

## God Has Not Abandoned His Purpose

Paul then explains something he does not want the Gentile believers to misunderstand:

> For I don’t desire you to be ignorant, brothers, of this mystery, so that you won’t be wise in your own conceits, that a partial hardening has happened to Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in, and so all Israel will be saved. Even as it is written, “There will come out of Zion the Deliverer, and he will turn away ungodliness from Jacob. This is my covenant with them, when I will take away their sins.”
>
> — Romans 11:25–27

Paul describes the hardening as partial and places an *until* within the account. He then announces Israel’s salvation and supports it with the promise of the Deliverer and the taking away of sins.

The salvation described is not God deciding that unbelief and sin no longer matter. The Deliverer turns away ungodliness; God takes away sins.

Paul distinguishes Israel’s present opposition to the Good News from their being beloved for the fathers’ sake in relation to election. He gives the reason:

> For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.
>
> — Romans 11:29

Here is the direct statement about God’s faithfulness that the chapter needs.

Israel’s present condition has not caused God to revoke the gifts and calling Paul is discussing. His purpose has not been erased by what the Gentile observer sees.

This statement belongs to its context. We should not turn it into a claim that every person is saved regardless of faith, especially after Paul has spoken so plainly about unbelief. But neither should we weaken its affirmation: God has not abandoned his calling concerning Israel.

His word has not come to nothing.

## What These Chapters Establish

Romans 9–11 does not give us permission to replace Paul’s careful argument with the general claim that every human failure affects experience alone.

He describes real unbelief, real hardening, branches broken off, and a warning against presumption. He also describes a remnant preserved by grace, Gentiles brought into blessing, God’s ability to graft in again, and a promised salvation grounded in his covenant.

The evidence for God’s faithfulness lies in that full account.

These chapters strengthen our understanding of the God who justifies. His promises are not defeated because events are more complex than we expected. His mercy is not a debt owed to human effort. His word must be understood according to what he actually promised.

The direct grounds for the believer’s standing remain those Paul has already established: redemption in Christ, justification by faith, reconciliation through his death, salvation by his life, and his present intercession.

Israel’s place in the argument confirms that we must take God’s stated purpose seriously. It does not replace those grounds with a loose comparison between a nation’s history and every detail of an individual’s life.

## From Him, Through Him, and to Him

Paul ends this discussion in worship:

> Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out!
>
> — Romans 11:33

He asks who has known the Lord’s mind, who has been his counselor, and who has first given to him so that repayment is owed.

The last question brings us back to the distinction between grace and wages. God is not settling an obligation created by our prior contribution.

Paul concludes:

> For of him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory for ever! Amen.
>
> — Romans 11:36

The argument has brought us to God’s mercy, wisdom, and faithfulness. Our part is not to invent a more secure foundation than the one he has provided through Christ.

We have followed the question of our standing through guilt, justification, reconciliation, union with Christ, life in the Spirit, and God’s faithfulness to his word. The answer rests on his action.

Paul will now appeal to believers by the mercies of God.

Those mercies are the foundation from which the appeal proceeds.

# Epilogue — By the Mercies of God

Paul’s letter continues beyond the argument we have followed. He speaks about serving, loving, giving, meeting obligations, and receiving people with whom we disagree.

Do these instructions introduce an unfinished requirement for acceptance? After explaining what God has accomplished through Christ, does Paul hand the remaining work back to us?

His opening appeal tells us how these chapters belong with what came before:

> Therefore I urge you, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your spiritual service.
>
> — Romans 12:1

**By the mercies of God.**

Paul appeals on the basis of the mercy he has explained. God justifies freely through the redemption in Christ Jesus. Those justified by faith have peace with him. Paul now calls these people to present themselves to God.

Their service does not supply something missing from the redemption. The distinction between a gift and a wage still stands. The presence of work in the believer’s life does not turn justification into payment for that work.

Paul describes believers as members of one body in Christ, with different gifts according to the grace given to them (Romans 12:4–6). Their service puts what they have received to use.

The same relationship between gift and response becomes especially clear when he addresses their disagreements.

> Don’t let him who eats despise him who doesn’t eat. Don’t let him who doesn’t eat judge him who eats, for God has accepted him.
>
> — Romans 14:3

One believer eats; another abstains. Paul brings God’s acceptance into the disagreement as a fact they must recognize.

He does not tell them to reach agreement so that God can finally decide whether to receive them. Their brother is someone God has accepted. That acceptance gives them a reason to refuse contempt and judgment.

Paul asks who they are to judge another’s servant, then points to God’s power to make him stand (Romans 14:4). The brother’s standing is not theirs to grant or withhold.

He carries the explanation further:

> For if we live, we live to the Lord. Or if we die, we die to the Lord. If therefore we live or die, we are the Lord’s.
>
> — Romans 14:8

Paul grounds this belonging in Christ’s death and resurrection: Christ died and lived again to be Lord of both the dead and the living (Romans 14:9).

Even in a dispute about food, the explanation reaches back to what Christ has done.

Belonging includes accountability. Paul says that each will give account to God, and he warns against harming a brother for whom Christ died (Romans 14:10–15). Acceptance does not make our treatment of another person unimportant. The person we are treating carelessly belongs to the Lord.

Then Paul states the connection plainly:

> Therefore accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you, to the glory of God.
>
> — Romans 15:7

Christ has accepted them. They are to receive one another accordingly.

The appeal is real, but it proceeds from acceptance already given. Their welcome does not purchase Christ’s welcome.

That connection remains as the letter turns to Paul’s ministry and the people who shared in it. He speaks of what Christ worked through him, then names fellow workers, helpers, and people who labored in the Lord (Romans 15:18; 16:1–16). Service has an honored place in the letter without becoming the grounds of justification.

The closing chapters therefore continue from God’s mercies. They do not introduce a second price for the gift.

The question this book has followed is what establishes our standing before God. Paul’s answer has been specific: redemption through Christ’s blood, righteousness counted apart from works, reconciliation through his death, and salvation by his life.

When Paul asks who can bring a charge, he answers that God justifies. When he asks who condemns, he points to Christ who died, rose, and intercedes for us.

The instructions that follow do not replace that answer.

At the beginning of this book, I recalled answering a question about acceptance by saying, “Because I gave you my life.” Romans has given us the grounds for understanding what was wrong with that answer. My service could not accomplish what Christ’s death and resurrection accomplished.

Our lives have a place in his service. They do not complete his sacrifice.

Our standing before God rests on the Christ through whom we have received reconciliation.

**It is settled.**
