Psalm 23

Notice how dynamic it is.

The shepherd:

The entire psalm is movement.

It is difficult to read Psalm 23 as a static description.

It feels like a journey.


That creates a fascinating sequence:

The shepherd leads the sheep.
The worshiper arrives at the house.
The question is asked: Who may ascend?
The qualified worshiper is described.
The gates open.
The King of Glory enters.

Before going further, I'd like to slow down over Psalm 24:3–6 because that section becomes crucial. The question is whether verses 3–6 are describing:

the worshiper entering,
a class of people,
the king himself,
or something more complex.

The text itself gives us some clues.

Psalm 24's Own Clues

Let's stay in the text.

The final scene is unusual.

The King of Glory is not enthroned.

He is arriving.

Not:

The King sits.

But:

Lift up your heads...

that the King of Glory may come in.

The psalm depicts an arrival.

That is one reason many readers have sensed an event behind the scene.

The psalm is not merely defining a king.

It is announcing one.

The psalm suddenly introduces Israel.

That is worth slowing down over.

The psalm could have ended:

But instead it adds:

Jacob.

The question becomes:

Why conclude the description of those who seek God's face with the name Jacob?

Because immediately afterward the psalm turns to:

Observation 3

The psalm never describes the reign.

Compare with Psalm 2.

Psalm 2 explicitly discusses:

Psalm 24 contains none of that.

It never describes what the King does after arriving.

The focus is entirely on His appearance.

Observation 4

The arrival creates the climax.

Suppose we stop at verse 6.

The psalm would read:

Who may ascend?

Who may stand?

Here is the qualified worshiper.

That would already be complete.

Yet verses 7–10 are added.

Why?

Because the psalm is not finished with the worshiper.

The climax is not the seeker.

The climax is the arrival of the King.

Observation 5

The King's identity is progressively revealed.

First question:

The psalm seems more interested in unveiling who is arriving than in describing His kingdom.

A Tentative Observation

If I had to choose between:

Option A

Psalm 24 is primarily about the LORD's kingship.

Option B

Psalm 24 is primarily about the arrival of the LORD as King.

The text currently seems to lean toward Option B.

Not because kingship is absent.

The King is explicitly identified.

But because the narrative energy is concentrated on:

"that the King of Glory may come in."

The psalm spends four verses describing an entrance.

It spends zero verses describing the reign that follows.

That suggests the arrival itself is the focal point.

Now the next question becomes very interesting:

So before identifying the event, we should probably investigate the destination. That may tell us whether this is:

a temple entrance,
a royal arrival,
or something larger.

What Psalm 24 Does Not Say

Psalm 24 never says:

The LORD becomes King.

It assumes He already is:

The King of Glory.

Likewise Zechariah 14 does not describe God becoming king in the sense of acquiring kingship.

Rather, His kingship becomes openly manifested.

The emphasis is on appearance.

Another Connection

Psalm 24 asks:

Who is this King of Glory?

Zechariah 14 answers:

The LORD will be king over all the earth.

Both passages end with the LORD openly recognized as King.

The Jerusalem Question

This is where your observation becomes important.

Psalm 24 never explicitly names Jerusalem.

Yet several clues point in that direction:

Earlier in the psalm:

Who may ascend the hill of the LORD?

Who may stand in His holy place?

The "hill of the LORD" naturally raises the question of Zion.

Then later:

gates

ancient doors

Then:

the King enters.

If we read Psalm 24 by itself, we should stop there.

But once we compare it with Zechariah 14, a pattern emerges:

Zechariah 14

The LORD comes to Jerusalem.

↓

The LORD is acknowledged as King.

Psalm 24

The King comes in.

↓

The LORD of hosts is identified as King.

The movement is remarkably similar.

One More Observation

Notice where Psalm 24 places the arrival.

Not at the beginning.

At the end.

The entire psalm moves toward it.

Likewise Zechariah 14 moves toward:

The LORD will be king over all the earth.

The kingship declaration is the climax.

A Possible Synthesis

Without claiming dependence, the two passages appear to share the same broad pattern:

Psalm 24

Qualified worshipers
↓
Seeking His face
↓
Gates opened
↓
The LORD arrives as King

Zechariah 14

Jerusalem under pressure
↓
The LORD intervenes
↓
The LORD arrives
↓
The LORD reigns as King

The strongest observation may be this:

Both passages culminate not merely in the LORD's authority, but in the LORD's manifest arrival and recognition as King.

That pushes Psalm 24 slightly away from being merely an abstract statement of divine kingship and slightly closer to an arrival scene. The question then becomes whether later prophets such as Zechariah are intentionally developing the same arrival motif, or whether both are drawing from a broader biblical expectation of the LORD coming to reign.
